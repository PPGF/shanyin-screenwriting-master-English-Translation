# Series Format Guide

## Core Characteristics

- **Episode Count & Runtime:** User-defined (common configurations: 8–24 episodes, 10–60 minutes each)  
- **Characters:** Full Ghost/Lie/Flaw/Want/Need; arc distributed across the season  
- **Structure:** Dual-layer nesting—in-episode structure \+ seasonal arc  
- **World:** Complex and layered release; requires information management  
- **Rhythm:** Each episode independently compelling \+ inter-episode hooks \+ seasonal progression  
- **Memory:** Requires continuity management system

\- World complexity: Simple (plug-and-play) / Medium (layered) / Complex (requires information release timeline)

## Series Types and Structural Strategy

Different series types have different weights between "episodic independence" and "seasonal continuity":

| Type | Episodic Independence | Seasonal Continuity | Reference Works |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Pure Anthology** | 100% | 0–10% (minor character growth) | *Black Mirror*, *Tales of the Unexpected* |
| **Anthology \+ Subplot** | 70–80% | 20–30% (subplot advances) | *Love, Death & Robots*, *The Missing* |
| **Semi-Serialized** | 40–60% | 40–60% (self-contained events serving the main arc) | *Breaking Bad*, *Stranger Things* |
| **Highly Serialized** | 10–20% | 80–90% (each episode is a chapter of one big story) | *Game of Thrones*, *Attack on Titan* |

Based on the user's selected type, adjust the weighting in all subsequent templates—anthology prioritizes per-episode quality; serialized prioritizes seasonal arc progression.

## Key Differences Between Series and Features

**A feature arc is one complete journey; a series arc is budget-driven.** The amount of change across N episodes must be allocated per episode. Change per episode needs "budget control"—earlier episodes cannot change the character too much, or later episodes have no room.

**Feature worldbuilding can be completed at once; series worldbuilding must be released in phases.** The first few episodes show only surface rules; the middle reveals operating logic; the season finale exposes the underlying truth. You need to know what the final episode will reveal when writing Episode 1—an "information release timeline" is required.

**A feature is one story; a series is N stories forming one larger story.** Each episode extracted individually must be a good, self-contained micro-story (episodic unit), while simultaneously laying bricks for the seasonal arc (continuity). When in conflict, single-episode quality takes priority.

## Dual-Layer Structure

### Layer 1: In-Episode Structure

Each episode needs a complete internal structure. Select based on per-episode runtime and user preference:

**Short episode (10–15 min)** → Four-Act (same as short format) **Medium episode (20–30 min)** → Extended Four-Act or compressed Save the Cat **Long episode (40–60 min)** → Save the Cat / Story Circle / McKee all viable

Below is the Four-Act in-episode template (proportionally scaled to any runtime):

\[COLD OPEN / OPENING HOOK (strongly recommended)\]

  Every episode needs an opening hook to grab the audience—not just Episode 1\.

  The cold open (teaser) is what plays before the title sequence.

  Its sole task is to make the audience decide to "keep watching."

  The cold open must be compelling audio-visual content; specific strategies:

  \- A high-tension scene that drops the audience directly into pressure (commercial series)

  \- A seemingly unrelated slice that pays off at the episode's end (mystery/anthology)

  \- Continuation and resolution of the previous episode's cliffhanger (serialized)

  \- A highly stylized passage anchoring the episode's emotional tone (arthouse series)

  Episode 1's cold open carries the heaviest load—simultaneously establishing world,

  characters, genre tone, and the seeds of the season's core conflict. The first two minutes

  of Episode 1 determine whether the audience will watch the entire season.

I. SETUP (approx. 15% of runtime)

   The episode's normal state. Can continue from the previous episode or jump to a new setting.

   Inciting incident—trigger of the episode's core conflict.

II. DEVELOPMENT (approx. 40% of runtime)

   Conflict escalates (at least two progressions).

   B Plot interweaves here.

   Seasonal subplot seeds scattered naturally in dialogue and behavior.

III. CLIMAX (approx. 25% of runtime)

   The peak of the episode's core conflict.

   Character makes a choice—this choice should embody or challenge the character's Lie.

IV. RESOLUTION \+ EPISODE-END HOOK (approx. 20% of runtime)

   The episode's story settles—show the "episode's new normal."

   Episode-end hook (cliffhanger or tease)—plants anticipation for the next episode's conflict.

   The hook need not be a big cliffhanger; it can be an unsettling detail, an unanswered question.

### Layer 2: Seasonal Arc

The seasonal arc is the overall story across the season's N episodes. Divided proportionally into four phases:

Phase 1: Setup (first 25% of episodes, i.e., E1 \~ E\[N×0.25\])

  Surface-level world rules presented.

  Protagonist's Want and Lie clearly established.

  Core relationship network completed.

  Seed of the season's core conflict planted.

Phase 2: Development (25%–65% of episodes)

  Seasonal core conflict escalates progressively.

  World's operating logic gradually revealed.

  Protagonist undergoes multiple false changes—appears to grow, but Lie remains intact.

  B Plots and subplots begin to interweave across episodes.

Phase 3: Crisis (65%–85% of episodes)

  Season's "All Is Lost" moment.

  Protagonist confronts their Ghost and Lie.

  Most important relationship is threatened or broken.

Phase 4: Climax and Resolution (final 15% of episodes)

  World's underlying truth revealed.

  Protagonist makes the season-level critical choice.

  Arc completes—show the character's new normal.

  Season finale can be conclusive or leave a hook for the next season.

**Proportional Adaptation Examples:**

| Total Episodes | Setup | Development | Crisis | Climax |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 8 episodes | E1–2 | E3–5 | E6–7 | E8 |
| 10 episodes | E1–3 | E4–6 | E7–8 | E9–10 |
| 12 episodes | E1–3 | E4–8 | E9–10 | E11–12 |
| 16 episodes | E1–4 | E5–10 | E11–13 | E14–16 |
| 24 episodes | E1–6 | E7–16 | E17–20 | E21–24 |

## Worldbuilding Information Release Timeline

Plan according to actual episode count N. Proportional guidelines:

Information Layer | Content | Release Position (by proportion) | Release Method

\------------------|---------|----------------------------------|----------------

Surface Rules | \[World's basic operating mode\] | First 10–15% (E1–E2 range) | Direct presentation

Surface Details | \[Interesting extensions of rules\] | 15–30% | Character encounters / B Plot

Operating Logic | \[Why the world is this way\] | 40–55% | Character investigation / discovery

Logic Extension | \[Deeper implications of logic\] | 55–75% | Leaked in relational dialogue

Underlying Truth | \[World's ultimate truth\] | Final 20% | Climax reveal

Foreshadowing Payoff | \[Earlier details gain new meaning\] | Final 1–2 episodes | Audience "ah-ha" moment

**Principles:**

- Each information layer requires at least 1–2 episodes of foreshadowing before release  
- Information released through character action and discovery—never via voice-over or subtitles  
- Releasing new information must change the audience's understanding of already-known information  
- Don't release too much new information in the same episode—at most one new layer per episode

## Series Essential Elements (Included by Default Unless User Specifies Otherwise)

The series format's length affords greater capacity than features. The following are standard for good series.

### Foreshadowing & Payoff (Cross-Episode)

Series foreshadowing is more powerful than in features—because the time span is longer, a detail seen in Episode 2 that pays off in Episode 8 has its impact amplified by the waiting time.

**Series Foreshadowing Categories:**

- **In-episode foreshadowing:** Planted and paid off within the same episode (same as feature)  
- **Short-range foreshadowing:** Pays off across 1–3 episodes. The audience remembers the plant when they see the payoff—the effect is "just as expected."  
- **Long-range foreshadowing:** Pays off across 5+ episodes. The audience has forgotten the plant when they see the payoff—the effect is "it was there from the start..."  
- **Season foreshadowing:** Planted in Episodes 1–2, paid off at the season finale. The most powerful—recontextualizes the entire season.

**Foreshadowing Density Suggestions:**

- At least 1 in-episode foreshadowing per episode  
- At least 1 short-range payoff every 3 episodes  
- At least 2–3 long-range foreshadowing across the season  
- At least 1 season foreshadowing

All foreshadowing should be tracked using the Continuity Management System's "Planting/Payoff Registration" table.

### Secondary Characters & Ensemble

**Series secondary characters are more developed than in features.** With more screen time, secondary characters are not merely functional—they can have their own mini-arcs.

**Secondary Character Functions (same as features):** Mirror, Catalyst, Thematic, Rhythmic.

**Additional Series Requirements:**

- Important secondary characters should have their own cross-episode micro-arcs (not necessarily as complete as the protagonist's, but with perceptible change)  
- A secondary character's micro-arc should intersect with the protagonist's arc at some point—influencing each other  
- Each episode may have 1–2 one-off characters (NPCs of that episode), but if the same type of NPC appears repeatedly across multiple episodes, consider consolidating them into one recurring secondary character  
- Ensemble series require a character relationship matrix, with relationship states updated each episode

### Subplots (Series-Grade)

Series subplots are more complex than in features, with more time to plant and reveal.

**Subplot Count Suggestion:** 1–3. Each subplot needs a clear start point (which episode begins planting) and end point (which episode reveals).

**Subplot Advancement Rules:**

- Subplots cannot advance in every episode—otherwise they wouldn't be "subplots"  
- Subplot hints should be hidden in scenes that have "another meaning on the surface"  
- When a subplot surfaces, it should change the audience's understanding of multiple previous episodes  
- Subplot advancement is managed using the Continuity Management System's "Subplot Progress Tracking" table

### Subplot Management (Series-Grade)

Series subplots are more complex than features—some subplots run across episodes:

- **In-episode A Plot:** The episode's core story  
- **In-episode B Plot:** The episode's secondary line, usually related to character arc  
- **Running Subplot:** Ongoing storyline across multiple episodes, not appearing every episode but remembered by the audience  
- **Season Arc:** The season's overall story, advanced in fragments each episode

**Interweaving Principles:**

- Each episode's A Plot must be independently complete  
- B Plot can resolve across 1–2 episodes  
- Running Subplot advances every 2–3 episodes; each advancement must carry weight  
- Season Arc advancement must not disrupt the episode's A Plot rhythm—if in conflict, cut Season Arc material  
- No more than 3 subplots (including Running Subplot) running simultaneously

## Runtime Estimation & Dual-Track Rhythm (Series-Grade)

### Per-Episode Runtime Budget

Each episode should have a runtime budget at the episode outline stage. Allocate proportionally across the Four-Act structure (see core-methodology.md Section 7), then refine per scene at the scene breakdown stage.

### Dual-Track Rhythm Labeling

Each scene should be labeled with both rhythm dimensions:

\[SCENE X: Location / Time / Visual Description\] (approx. X min)

  Plot Rhythm: Loose/Medium/Tight

  Emotional Rhythm: Light/Medium/Heavy

### Intra-Episode Rhythm Check

- No more than 3 consecutive scenes with the same rhythm state within an episode  
- Climax segments (Tight Plot \+ Heavy Emotion) should occupy 20%–30% of the episode's total runtime—too short is unsatisfying; too long causes fatigue  
- Each episode should have at least one "Loose Plot \+ Heavy Emotion" or "Tight Plot \+ Light Emotion" misaligned passage—these are the most resonant moments

### Inter-Episode Rhythm Check

In addition to emotional tone (see Inter-Episode Rhythm Management), also check:

- Whether the average plot intensity of adjacent episodes has contrast—cannot have consecutive high-intensity episodes  
- Whether the average emotional intensity of adjacent episodes has contrast—cannot have consecutive heavy-emotion episodes  
- Whether the season's mid-section "breathing episodes" truly "breathe"—both plot and emotion need to come down

## Series Workflow (Two Phases)

Series creation is divided into two phases: first complete "Season Planning \+ Episode Outlines" to establish the season's skeleton and each episode's concept; then proceed episode by episode through the eight-step workflow to write individual scripts.

### Phase One: Season Planning and Episode Outlines

This phase is done only once, but may require multiple rounds of iteration. The goal is to stabilize the season's skeleton before writing any full script.

#### Step A: Season Planning

1. **Series Configuration Confirmation:** Episode count, runtime, series type (see Configuration section at the top)  
2. **Season Premise:** What is the overall story of this season? Summarize in one sentence (logline level)  
3. **Season Rough Logline Draft:** One-page season story summary (300–500 words), giving the user a basic sense of the season's direction and tone. Can be revised later  
4. **Protagonist's Seasonal Arc:** From State A to State B; what is the Lie?  
5. **Backstory and World** (following the "cross-section principle"—the world has been running long before the story begins):  
   - Period Setting: What era/social environment does this story take place in? What era characteristics affect the characters?  
   - Protagonist Backstory: Each major character's key life events before the story begins (especially the Ghost event)  
   - Relationship History: Relationship history between major characters before the story begins  
   - World Design: Three-layer model (Surface Rules/Operating Logic/Underlying Truth) \+ Information Release Timeline  
   - Cross-Section Selection: Why does Episode 1 cut in at this moment?  
6. **Arc Budget Allocation:** First draft of the arc budget table for protagonist and major supporting characters  
7. **Subplot Planning:** Start and end points of cross-episode subplots (if any)

#### Step B: Episode Outlines

**This is the most critical step in series creation.** Before writing any episode's script, complete outlines for all N episodes.

**Why episode outlines must precede scripts:**

- Episode outlines are the "map"—setting out without a map leads to getting lost  
- Only by viewing the entire season holistically can you judge whether the rhythmic wave is reasonable  
- Arc budget consumption can only be tested with a global view  
- Planting and payoffs require coordination between episodes—not mapping them out leads to omissions

### Phase Two: Per-Episode Script (Eight-Step Workflow)

After episode outlines are approved, proceed episode by episode through the eight-step workflow.

**Note:** Steps 2 (Rough Logline) and 4 (Backstory & World) for the series were already completed in Phase One's season planning. When writing individual episodes, these two steps become "confirm against the episode outline"—unless the actual development deviates from the outline and requires updating.

**Step 1 (Premise) Additional Requirements:**

- Core dramatic action must align with the episode outline  
- Check whether the episode concept has rhythmic contrast with adjacent episodes

**Step 2 (Rough Logline) — Confirm against outline:**

- Review the episode's logline and structural summary from the episode outline; confirm they still hold  
- If previous episodes deviated from the outline requiring adjustments, update this episode's logline accordingly

**Step 3 (Characters) Additional Requirements:**

- Secondary characters appearing in this episode need their functions labeled (Mirror/Catalyst/Thematic/Rhythmic)  
- If recurring secondary characters appear, check their micro-arc progress

**Step 4 (Backstory/World) — Confirm against outline:**

- Review the season planning's worldbuilding; confirm whether this episode needs to release a new information layer  
- If new characters debut this episode, supplement their backstory

**Step 5 (Structural Outline) Additional Requirements:**

- In-episode structure \+ episode-end hook design  
- **Opening Hook Design:** Each episode's cold open/first scene must have a sufficiently strong visual hook (see core-methodology.md Section 3.5)—Episode 1's opening carries the heaviest load  
- Label this episode's subplot advancement (if any)  
- Label this episode's arc budget consumption  
- Check against the episode outline—if deviation occurs, assess cascading impact  
- **Refine runtime budget**

**Step 6 (Scene Breakdown) Additional Requirements:**

- **Each scene labeled with estimated runtime and dual-track rhythm state**  
- Check in-episode rhythm: no more than 3 consecutive scenes with the same rhythm state  
- Sum scene runtimes and verify  
- **If the episode exceeds 6 scenes, trigger one in-episode memory checkpoint after Step 6**

**Step 7 (Scene Writing) Additional Requirements:**

- **During single-episode writing, trigger one in-episode memory checkpoint approximately every 6 scenes**  
- Write with the checkpoint as reference to confirm consistency  
- Backstory information naturally scattered through dialogue and visual cues

**Step 8 (Script Doctor) Additional Requirements:**

- Continuity audit: check for contradictions with previously completed episodes  
- Update all tracking tables (see Continuity Management System)  
- Check episode-end hook effectiveness  
- **Opening Hook Quality Check:** Is this episode's cold open compelling enough?  
- **Foreshadowing Audit:** Have all foreshadowing planted this episode been registered? Have all payoffs due this episode been made?  
- **Rhythm Diagnosis:** Use scene runtime summary to check the episode's rhythm  
- **Secondary Character Audit**  
- Assess cascading impact if deviating from the outline  
- **After the episode is completed, MUST trigger an inter-episode memory checkpoint** (see core-methodology.md Section 10\)

## Inter-Episode Rhythm Management

Not every episode can have the same emotional tone. The rhythm needs waves.

### Emotional Tone Type Library

Select one dominant tone for each episode from the following (mixing allowed, but a clear dominant should exist):

- **Setup type:** Audience adapts to the world, gets to know characters (typically for early episodes)  
- **Light type:** Strong comedic feel, character interaction-focused, audience builds affinity  
- **Tension type:** Core conflict escalates, pressure increases  
- **Breathing type:** Pace slows, character relationships deepen, provides emotional anchors  
- **Turning type:** Major event occurs, landscape changes  
- **Pressure type:** Internal and external pressures tighten continuously, almost no respite  
- **Collapse type:** All is lost, lowest point  
- **Dark Night type:** Character confronts Ghost and Lie directly  
- **Counterattack type:** Character counterattacks with new understanding  
- **Climax type:** Seasonal core conflict resolves

### Rhythmic Wave Principles

- Cannot have 3+ consecutive episodes of the same tone  
- Tension/Pressure episodes need at least 1 episode of Breathing or Light following  
- Collapse and Dark Night can be consecutive (forming a "low valley"), but must be contrasted before and after  
- The season's mid-section (40%–60% position) is suitable for a Turning type—changing the direction of the second half  
- Breathing episodes are not filler—their function is deepening character relationships and advancing B Plots

### Reference Rhythm Template (by proportion of total episodes)

First 10%: Setup

10–20%: Light/Setup

20–30%: First Tension

30–40%: Breathing

40–50%: Turning (mid-section major event)

50–60%: Transition \+ Escalation

60–70%: Pressure

70–75%: Breathing or False Climax

75–85%: Collapse/Dark Night

85–95%: Counterattack

Final 5–10%: Climax \+ Resolution

The above is only a reference—the specific rhythm should be adjusted based on the story itself. The key is creating emotional undulation.

## Series Common Pitfalls

- ❌ Skipping episode outlines and writing individual episodes directly → Must map the entire season before writing  
- ❌ Sacrificing per-episode quality for the seasonal arc → Each episode extracted individually must be good  
- ❌ Information released too quickly (worldbuilding completed in first few episodes) → Layer release  
- ❌ Arc budget out of control (character changes too much in early episodes, leaving nowhere to go later) → Strictly follow budget proportions  
- ❌ Continuity errors (character behavior contradicts previous episodes) → Check tracking tables before writing each episode  
- ❌ Episode-end hooks too forced (manufacturing conflict for suspense) → Hook must be a natural extension of the episode's story  
- ❌ Every episode's structure too similar (audience can predict rhythm) → Use inter-episode rhythm management to create variation  
- ❌ Too many running subplots; audience can't keep track → No more than 3 running simultaneously  
- ❌ Episode outline is only a one-sentence summary → Outline must contain complete in-episode structural summary, runtime budget, dual-track rhythm, and season connections  
- ❌ Deviating from the outline when writing individual episodes without updating subsequent episodes → Any deviation requires assessing cascading impact on later episodes  
- ❌ No runtime estimation leading to compressed climax or overlong setup → Start runtime budgeting from the episode outline stage  
- ❌ Multiple consecutive episodes of Tight Plot \+ Heavy Emotion → Audience fatigue; use dual-track rhythm to check inter-episode contrast  
- ❌ Foreshadowing planted but payoff forgotten → Audit each entry with Planting/Payoff Registration  
- ❌ Secondary characters appear only when needed, disappear when not → Secondary characters need their own logic of existence  
- ❌ Plot rhythm and emotional rhythm always synchronized → Deliberate misalignment (Loose Plot \+ Heavy Emotion) is an advanced technique

# Feature Format Guide (90-Minute Film)

## Core Characteristics

- **Runtime:** 75–120 minutes  
- **Characters:** Protagonist has full Ghost/Lie/Flaw/Want/Need; supporting characters carry B-Story  
- **Structure:** Select methodology based on subject matter (see Structure Selection Guide below)  
- **Arc:** True transformation after multiple false changes  
- **World:** Can be complex, revealed in layers  
- **Rhythm:** Requires "breathing room"—alternating tension and release, not sustained high density

## Key Differences Between Features and Shorts

**A short is a sprint; a feature is a marathon.** Shorts win on density; features need rhythmic undulation—quiet scenes for emotional digestion, comic relief to modulate tension. 90 minutes of sustained high density will exhaust the audience.

**A short tells an event; a feature tells a journey.** The protagonist in a feature experiences not just one setback but a series of escalating trials, each revealing deeper truths about the character.

**Subtext in shorts lives in dialogue; subtext in features lives in scene sequences.** The juxtaposition of two scenes itself conveys meaning—a wedding immediately followed by a funeral needs no dialogue; the edit itself is subtext.

## Character Depth Design (Feature-Grade)

Feature character design adds three dimensions beyond the short:

### Ghost (Backstory Trauma)

A past event that shaped the character's current worldview and behavioral patterns. The Ghost need not be shown in full—it can be implied solely through the character's reactions. Example: The character never stands in the rain—the audience doesn't know why until Act 2 reveals they lost someone in the rain.

### Lie (The Character's False Belief)

A false belief formed by the Ghost. The Lie drives the character's Want. Example: Ghost \= "betrayed by the most trusted person" → Lie \= "can't trust anyone" → Want \= "complete the mission alone."

### Flaw (Character Defect)

The behavioral externalization of the Lie. The Flaw is a pattern of self- and other-destructive behavior. Example: Lie \= "can't trust anyone" → Flaw \= "pushes away everyone who tries to get close."

**Complete Character Arc Chain:** Ghost → Lie → Flaw → Want (misguided pursuit) → Series of trials → Facing Need (true requirement) → Critical choice (cling to Lie or embrace Need) → New normal

## Structure Selection Guide

Select structure methodology based on subject matter and creative intent. Mixing is allowed—e.g., using Story Circle as the skeleton and Save the Cat's beats for refinement.

| Subject Matter/Intent | Recommended Methodology | Reason |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Commercial genre films (action, thriller, comedy, horror) | **Save the Cat 15 Beats** | Beats precise to the minute—suited for genre films requiring strict rhythm control |
| Coming-of-age/adventure/transformation | **Dan Harmon Story Circle** | Eight-step circle naturally fits the "leave home → adventure → return" hero's journey variant |
| High character complexity, theme-driven dramas | **McKee Story Principle** | Centers on value shifts and antagonism—suited for deep drama |
| Character-driven arthouse | **Internal Beat Sheet** (see style variants below) | Beats are shifts in internal state, not external events |
| Mixed/uncertain | Ask user preference; default to Story Circle as skeleton | Story Circle is sufficiently universal and accessible |

If the user has no clear preference, suggest based on the described story type with brief reasoning, then confirm before proceeding.

## Structure Templates

### Template A: Save the Cat 15 Beats (Commercial)

For: Genre films, high-concept commercial films, strong plot-driven stories. Core logic: Each beat has a precise time window—the entire piece operates as a rhythmic machine.

ACT ONE (approx. 25 min)

1\. Opening Image

   First shot/scene. Shows the character's "old world"—state before change.

   Contrasts with Final Image.

   \*\*This is not just an "establishing shot"—it must be compelling audio-visual content.\*\*

   Commercial: a high-density scene simultaneously showing ability, personality, and world rules.

   Arthouse: a stylized passage anchoring the film's visual identity.

   Either way, Opening Image alone should be worth watching.

2\. Theme Stated (approx. minute 5\)

   A character (usually not the protagonist) inadvertently states the theme in dialogue.

   The protagonist does not yet understand its true meaning.

3\. Set-Up (minutes 1–10)

   Show protagonist's daily life, flaws, world.

   Lay out all characters and relationships to be used later.

   Make the audience care about the protagonist (sympathy or identification).

4\. Catalyst (approx. minute 12\)

   The event that breaks the balance. Irreversible change.

   The protagonist's normal life is shattered—a letter, a phone call, an accident.

5\. Debate (minutes 12–25)

   Protagonist hesitates in the face of change. Accept the challenge or not?

   Reveals the Flaw—precisely because of the Lie, the protagonist resists change.

ACT TWO, FIRST HALF (approx. 30 min)

6\. Break into Two (approx. minute 25\)

   Protagonist makes a decision and enters the "new world."

   This is an active choice, not being pushed.

7\. B Story

   Introduce B Story—usually a new relationship (love, friendship, mentor).

   B Story carries the thematic argument. A Plot asks "can the protagonist achieve the goal,"

   B Plot asks "can the protagonist grow."

8\. Fun and Games (minutes 30–55)

   The section with the most trailer footage.

   Protagonist attempts to solve new problems with old methods in the new world.

   Both successes and setbacks, but overall lighter in tone.

9\. Midpoint (approx. minute 55\)

   A Plot and B Plot converge here.

   False victory (looks like a win but isn't) or false defeat (looks like a loss but is a turning point).

   Stakes escalate—from personal to larger.

ACT TWO, SECOND HALF (approx. 30 min)

10\. Bad Guys Close In (minutes 55–75)

    Internal and external pressures tighten simultaneously.

    External: villain/obstacles escalate. Internal: team fractures, self-doubt emerges.

    The protagonist's Flaw begins to exact real costs.

11\. All Is Lost (approx. minute 75\)

    Lowest point. The protagonist loses what matters most.

    "Whiff of death"—literal or metaphorical death.

    Old methods fail completely.

12\. Dark Night of the Soul (minutes 75–85)

    Protagonist confronts their Lie and Ghost directly.

    Not awakened by someone else—the protagonist sees the truth in the ruins themselves.

    This is the critical threshold of arc transformation.

ACT THREE (approx. 15 min)

13\. Break into Three (approx. minute 85\)

    Protagonist reaches clarity—not an upgraded version of old methods, but something entirely new.

    A Plot and B Plot insights converge here.

14\. Finale (minutes 85–105)

    Five-step climax method:

    a. Gathering the team

    b. Executing the plan

    c. High tower surprise—plan fails

    d. Dig deep down—respond to the crisis with the new, evolved self

    e. Execution of the new plan

15\. Final Image

    Contrasts with Opening Image.

    Shows the character's "new normal"—state after change.

    No need to explain what the character learned. One image is worth a thousand words.

### Template B: Dan Harmon Story Circle

For: Coming-of-age, adventure, transformation stories—where the character's outer and inner journeys advance in parallel. Core logic: The character leaves the comfort zone, enters the unknown, pays a price to gain something, and returns changed. The beauty of the circle lies in "returning to the start but already different."

Story Circle Eight Steps:

1\. YOU — Character in comfort zone

   Establish the character's normal state. The audience needs to understand "what this person is usually like."

   The comfort zone isn't necessarily happy—it can be a painful but familiar state.

   \*\*Opening requirement same as STC: this "normal state" display itself must be compelling\*\*

   \*\*audio-visual content, not just flat exposition. Either use a tense routine task to showcase the character,\*\*

   \*\*or use a stylized passage to immediately immerse the audience in this world.\*\*

2\. NEED — Character develops desire

   The character realizes (or is forced to face) what they lack.

   This Need can be external (a mission, a goal),

   or internal (a dissatisfaction, a longing).

3\. GO — Character enters unfamiliar territory

   Cross the threshold, leave the comfort zone.

   This is an irreversible decision—no turning back.

4\. SEARCH — Character searches, adapts, struggles

   Trial and error in the new environment. Encounters allies and obstacles.

   Character applies old methods to new problems, sometimes works, sometimes doesn't.

   Corresponds to Save the Cat's Fun and Games.

5\. FIND — Character gets what they wanted

   Corresponds to Midpoint. Character achieves the surface goal.

   But this "achievement" brings unexpected costs.

6\. TAKE — Character pays the price for what they got

   After getting what they wanted, the cost begins to manifest.

   This is the bottom of the circle—the character is farthest from the comfort zone.

   Internal and external pressures are maximal; the old self begins to crumble.

7\. RETURN — Character returns to familiar territory

   Character returns (physically or psychologically) to the starting point.

   But the character has changed—the old comfort zone no longer fits.

8\. CHANGE — Character has changed

   Show the new normal after change.

   Contrast with Step 1: same person, same place, different state.

**Story Circle Unique Advantages:**

- Inherent symmetry—first four steps and last four steps mirror each other  
- Step 3 (GO) and Step 7 (RETURN) are symmetrical: departure and return  
- Step 4 (SEARCH) and Step 6 (TAKE) are symmetrical: seeking and paying the price  
- This symmetry gives the story a sense of "completeness"  
- More flexible than Save the Cat—not bound to precise minute counts  
- Particularly suited for character journeys and transformation stories

**Correspondence with Save the Cat:**

| Story Circle | Save the Cat |
| :---- | :---- |
| 1\. YOU | Opening Image \+ Set-Up |
| 2\. NEED | Catalyst \+ Debate |
| 3\. GO | Break into Two |
| 4\. SEARCH | Fun and Games |
| 5\. FIND | Midpoint |
| 6\. TAKE | Bad Guys Close In \+ All Is Lost \+ Dark Night |
| 7\. RETURN | Break into Three |
| 8\. CHANGE | Finale \+ Final Image |

### Template C: McKee Story Principle

For: High character complexity dramas, theme-first works, deep dramatic tension. Core logic: Story is "value shift"—every scene must produce an irreversible positive-to-negative or negative-to-positive reversal of some life value (love/hate, freedom/confinement, truth/lie, etc.). A scene without value shift is a dead scene.

McKee Core Framework:

1\. Controlling Idea

   A one-sentence thematic statement for the entire piece.

   Format: \[Value\] \+ \[Cause\].

   Example: "Justice prevails because the protagonist is willing to sacrifice everything they cherish."

   Example: "Love collapses in the face of utilitarianism because self-interest corrodes sincerity."

   The Controlling Idea determines the ending—it is the final conclusion the entire piece argues.

2\. Value Charge

   Every scene has a core value (life/death, love/hate, hope/despair, etc.).

   The value begins in some state (positive or negative).

   The scene must end with a reversal (positive → negative, or negative → positive).

   If the value state at a scene's end is the same as at its beginning—cut the scene.

3\. The Gap

   When the character takes action, there is a discrepancy between expected and actual results.

   This discrepancy is "the Gap"—the character does A expecting B but receives C.

   The larger the Gap, the stronger the dramatic tension.

   Good scripts deepen the Gap layer by layer—each time the character thinks they've learned,

   the next Gap is deeper and more unexpected than the last.

4\. Inciting Incident

   The event that irrevocably disrupts the character's life balance.

   Can be causal (caused by character's actions) or coincidental (caused by external forces).

   The Inciting Incident creates the central question of the entire piece—unanswerable until the end.

5\. Progressive Complications

   Conflict must escalate progressively. Each failed attempt must cost more than the previous.

   McKee's key requirement: don't only escalate external difficulty—

   the character's inner predicament must deepen simultaneously.

   "If your character faces the same level of difficulty at minute 60 as at minute 30,

   your story has stalled."

6\. Crisis

   The character faces an irreversible dilemma:

   \- The best bad choice (lesser of two evils)

   \- The irreconcilable choice (can only take one of two goods)

   This choice must be a true dilemma—both options carry weight.

   If one option is clearly better, it's not a true crisis.

7\. Climax

   The enactment of the Crisis choice. The character makes the choice and bears the consequences.

   The Climax must be irreversible—after this choice is made,

   neither character nor world can return to the pre-Climax state.

8\. Resolution

   The new order after the Climax. A brief display of the changed world.

   The Controlling Idea is finally confirmed or refuted here.

**McKee Method Unique Value:**

- Forces every scene to have value shift—eliminates dead scenes  
- The "Gap" concept makes conflict progress naturally—not artificially escalating difficulty, but deepening naturally through the discrepancy between expectation and reality  
- Controlling Idea makes theme concrete and testable—not vague "about courage," but an arguable proposition  
- Particularly suited for dramas requiring deep character excavation and thematic expression

**McKee Self-Check Points:**

- [ ] Is the Controlling Idea clear and arguable?  
- [ ] Does every scene have value shift?  
- [ ] Does the Gap deepen progressively?  
- [ ] Is the Crisis a true dilemma?  
- [ ] Is the Climax irreversible?

### Template D: Arthouse Internal Beat Sheet

For: Character-driven films, auteur cinema, stories not reliant on strong plot.

Arthouse beats are not external events but nodes of internal state change. External events may be small (a glance, casual talk, a walk)—but they trigger internal earthquakes.

#### General Internal Beat Structure

1\. Normalcy and the Crack

   The character's daily life. But some detail hints at unease beneath the surface.

   The arthouse setup doesn't rush—it lingers in the character's world,

   building atmosphere through sensory details (sound, light, objects).

   \*\*Opening requirement: though not pursuing commercial tension, this "normalcy" must\*\*

   \*\*be a passage representing the film's style and tone—making the audience feel within\*\*

   \*\*the first two minutes that "this film's world is different from what I see every day."\*\*

   \*\*A highly stylized long take, a carefully designed daily ritual,\*\*

   \*\*a quiet image that feels "not quite right"—all can serve as arthouse hooks.\*\*

2\. Disturbance

   A small event breaks the character's self-deception.

   The arthouse catalyst is usually not a dramatic big event, but a subtle moment

   that makes it "impossible to continue pretending everything is normal."

3\. Wandering and Avoidance

   The character tries to return to the old life, but the crack has already appeared.

   Plenty of "in-between moments"—character alone, spacing out, doing trivial things.

   These in-betweens are not filler—they show what the character is avoiding facing.

4\. Confrontation

   The character is forced to face what they've been avoiding.

   Could be a quiet conversation, could be a reunion after a long time,

   could simply be standing somewhere and finding themselves crying.

5\. Choice or Acceptance

   The arthouse climax is usually not about "what was done," but "what was accepted."

   The character either accepts a painful truth or decides to continue living in the lie.

   Both endings work—what matters is the weight of the choice.

6\. Aftershock

   The ending is not an answer—it's a new question.

   The arthouse Final Image is usually open—the character has changed,

   but the consequences of the change have not yet arrived.

#### Style Variants: Director/Genre Rhythm Approaches

Based on the user's aesthetic direction or reference films, select the corresponding style variant. Variants do not change the six-step internal beat skeleton—they adjust each step's "texture": how long to linger, what means to convey information, the proportion of negative space.

**Variant 1: Kore-eda Style (Everyday Flow)** Reference works: *Still Walking*, *Shoplifters*, *Nobody Knows*

Characteristics:

- Extremely slow setup; first 30 minutes are almost entirely everyday life—eating, chores, small talk. Plot hides in life's details.  
- No clear "inciting incident." The crack is not opened by one event but slowly seeps through accumulated daily life.  
- Confrontation is not direct conflict, but emotion that suddenly leaks in an otherwise ordinary moment—a sentence cut off mid-speech, a sudden quickening of steps, a glance at a place that's no longer occupied.  
- Ending lands on an extremely ordinary action—clearing dishes, turning off lights, walking home. The world hasn't changed, but the audience knows something is different.  
- Dialogue is extremely mundane; characters never discuss "big things," but the shadow of big things is always present.  
- Rhythm is like breathing—even, slow, without abrupt changes.

**Variant 2: Wong Kar-wai Style (Emotional Flow)** Reference works: *Chungking Express*, *In the Mood for Love*, *Happy Together*

Characteristics:

- Narrative does not pursue causality—scene connections are emotional, not logical. A scene doesn't need to "continue" from the previous one; it just needs to echo or contrast emotionally.  
- Extensive repeating visual motifs—the same space shot repeatedly at different times, the same action repeated by different characters. Repetition creates the texture of time.  
- Characters voice internal thoughts in voice-over, but there is a gap between what the narration says and what the image shows—speaking of one thing, showing another. The gap is subtext.  
- No traditional climax. The story reaches emotional saturation at some point, then quietly wraps up.  
- Time is the protagonist—jumping timelines, expired dates, phones that no longer ring.

**Variant 3: Ozu Style (Still Life Flow)** Reference works: *Tokyo Story*, *The Flavor of Green Tea over Rice*, *Late Spring*

Characteristics:

- Very low camera position, fixed shots, camera almost never moves. The image itself is still; only the people in it move.  
- "Pillow shots"—empty shots inserted between scenes: corridors, rooftops, distant chimneys. These empty shots don't narrate; they create pauses for emotional sedimentation.  
- Family relationships are the eternal theme. Conflict comes not from external events but from the unbridgeable gap between generations—parents must eventually accept their children's departure; children can never fully understand their parents' sacrifice.  
- Characters are extremely polite and restrained on the surface, but restraint itself is the greatest subtext.  
- The ending is usually a form of "acceptance"—neither joy nor sorrow, but a quiet "so it is."

**Variant 4: Richard Linklater Style (Dialogue Flow)** Reference works: *Before Sunrise* trilogy, *Dazed and Confused*

Characteristics:

- Almost entirely dialogue-driven. Dialogue is not a tool; dialogue is the story itself.  
- Dialogue rhythm mimics real conversation—it digresses, circles back, turns serious then funny again.  
- Characters naturally reveal themselves through chat—not through "confession," but through what they choose to talk about, what they avoid, where they linger one second longer.  
- Time and space constraints create intimacy—two people must decide what's worth saying in limited time.  
- The climax is one sentence said at the right moment—after all the build-up, when a certain line is spoken, the audience knows "everything is different."  
- The ending is usually open—the conversation ends, but the relationship is still in the air.

**Variant 5: Bong Joon-ho Style (Genre Hybrid)** Reference works: *Parasite*, *Memories of Murder*, *Mother*

Characteristics:

- The skeleton is strict commercial structure (Save the Cat or Story Circle can serve as the base), but social critique and dark humor are inserted beneath the genre skin.  
- First half and second half can be completely different genres—*Parasite*'s first half is comedy, second half is thriller. Genre mutation itself is part of the narrative.  
- Space as metaphor—characters' physical positions (up/down, inside/outside, above ground/underground) directly correspond to social positions.  
- Ambiguity of details—the same object or action in the first half is a comedic prop, in the second half becomes a horror/tragic prop.  
- The ending is an emotional bomb wrapped in irony—surface-level deadpan joke, interior despair.  
- Recommend this variant if the user's direction is "genre film shell with social-issue core."

**How to Use Style Variants:**

1. Use the General Internal Beat Sheet to build the skeleton  
2. Select a variant based on the user's reference films or aesthetic preference  
3. Fill each step's specific writing with the variant's "texture"  
4. Variants can be mixed—e.g., use Kore-eda's rhythm with Wong Kar-wai's voice-over misalignment technique

**Arthouse General Self-Check:**

- [ ] Does every "quiet scene" convey the character's internal state?  
- [ ] Is atmosphere built through sensory details rather than dialogue?  
- [ ] Does character change come from internal rather than external event drivers?  
- [ ] Does the ending leave room for aftershock?  
- [ ] Is there a unifying visual/emotional motif throughout?  
- [ ] Does it match the selected style variant's texture?

## Subplot Interweaving (Feature-Specific)

Features typically have 2–3 storylines running simultaneously:

- **A Plot (main):** The protagonist's external journey—pursuing Want, facing obstacles  
- **B Plot (subplot/B Story):** Usually a relationship, carrying the thematic argument. B Plot's progress reflects the protagonist's internal change  
- **C Plot (third line, optional):** Rhythmic counterpoint, or reflects theme from another angle

**Interweaving Principles:**

- A/B lines converge at the Midpoint  
- B Plot's progress should mirror A Plot—when A Plot falters, B Plot provides consolation or counter-evidence  
- Don't let subplots become independent mini-stories—each line must ultimately serve the protagonist's arc

## Feature Essential Elements (Included by Default Unless User Specifies Otherwise)

Features have enough runtime to accommodate complexity. The following elements are not optional in features—they are standard for good cinema. If a 90-minute script lacks any of these, proactively alert the user and suggest supplementation.

### Foreshadowing & Payoff

**What foreshadowing is:** Planting a detail (dialogue, prop, action, image) unobtrusively early in the story, then having that detail reappear with greater significance later.

**Foreshadowing Layers:**

- **Prop foreshadowing:** An object appears ordinary early, becomes crucial later. Example: a lighter casually bought becomes the tool for igniting an escape signal in Act 3\.  
- **Dialogue foreshadowing:** A line that's casual talk early gains new meaning later. Example: "What I fear most is silence"—character description in Act 1, echoed in Act 3 when the character faces solitude.  
- **Behavior foreshadowing:** A habitual action hints at the Ghost. Example: a character always leaves the door slightly ajar—later revealed they were once locked in a room.  
- **Structural foreshadowing:** A seemingly unrelated scene later proves to contain key information. Example: a passerby's conversation in the opening turns out to reveal the true puppet master.

**Foreshadowing Rules:**

- First appearance must not be too obvious—audience shouldn't realize "this is foreshadowing"  
- Every foreshadowing must have a payoff—foreshadowing without payoff is a broken thread  
- Payoff should create an "ah-ha" feeling—the payoff should alter the audience's understanding of earlier information  
- Features need at least 3–5 foreshadowing/payoff pairs across different layers

### Secondary Characters & Ensemble Design

**Secondary characters are not backdrop.** Every speaking character in a feature should have a functional reason for existing.

**Four Functions of Secondary Characters:**

- **Mirror character:** Reflects an aspect of the protagonist. They walked a path the protagonist didn't—"if the protagonist had made different choices, they'd become this person."  
- **Catalytic character:** External force pushing the protagonist to change. Mentor, tempter, challenger.  
- **Thematic character:** Embodies one position in the thematic argument. B Story characters usually serve this function.  
- **Rhythmic character:** Provides comic relief, emotional modulation. Function is to give the audience breathing room during tense passages.

**Secondary Character Design Rules:**

- Each secondary character should serve at least one function, preferably two  
- Characters without function should be merged or removed—two characters with the same function can be merged into one  
- Secondary characters don't need full Ghost/Lie/Flaw, but need a clear Want and at least one contradictory trait  
- Secondary character dialogue style should be distinctly differentiated from the protagonist—audience should be able to identify who's speaking even with names covered

**Ensemble Additional Requirements:** If the story has 3+ equally important characters (ensemble structure), additionally design:

- Relationship matrix—state of relationship between each pair of characters  
- Perspective-switching rules—when to follow whose perspective, whether switches have rhythm  
- Each character has their own Want/Need, but all pursuits converge on the same theme

### Subplot Design (Feature-Grade)

Subplots are storylines running beneath the surface—the audience may not notice on first viewing, but on second viewing will see "it was being laid from the very beginning."

**Difference Between Subplot and Subplot:**

- Subplots are visible secondary storylines—they explicitly advance  
- Subplots are hidden beneath the surface—they operate through foreshadowing, details, casual dialogue until surfacing at some point

**Subplot Design Method:**

1. First determine the Subplot's end point—what will be revealed when it surfaces  
2. Reverse-engineer—how many hints need to be planted earlier to make the revelation work  
3. Each hint must have surface meaning—on first viewing the audience reads the surface meaning; on second viewing, the subplot meaning  
4. The Subplot surfaces usually in the Climax or Resolution

**Feature Subplot Count Suggestion:** 1–2. Too many make the story overly complex.

## World Layered Reveal (Feature-Grade)

Short worldbuilding is "plug-and-play," but features can have layered complexity:

### Three-Layer Model

Surface Rules (shown in first 30 min)

  The world surface that the audience and characters encounter first.

  "What is the basic operating mode of this world."

Operating Logic (shown in Act 2\)

  The deeper mechanism behind the surface rules.

  "Why is this world this way."

  Usually revealed through the character's deeper exploration or the B Story character's perspective.

Underlying Truth (shown in Climax)

  The ultimate truth of the entire world.

  "What this world truly is."

  This reveal should change the audience's understanding of all preceding events.

**Key Principle:** Each layer's reveal should emerge naturally through character action and discovery—never through voice-over, subtitles, or characters sitting down to "explain."

## Feature Eight-Step Workflow

Features use the same eight-step workflow skeleton as shorts, but with higher depth and requirements at each step.

**Step 1 (Premise) Additional Requirements:**

- Beyond core dramatic action, also establish the **Thematic Argument**  
- The Thematic Argument is an arguable proposition; A Plot and B Plot represent opposing sides

**Step 2 (Rough Logline) Additional Requirements:**

- Feature logline should be a **one-page Treatment** (300–500 words), not just one paragraph  
- Treatment should include: protagonist's core predicament, inciting event direction, Midpoint turn direction, Climax general shape, Resolution emotional tone  
- If there is a B Story, the Treatment should convey the presence of both lines  
- Treatment's tone must convey the piece's genre quality—reading it should tell "what kind of film this is"

**Step 3 (Characters) Additional Requirements:**

- Add Ghost/Lie/Flaw dimensions  
- Design B Story character (carrying the opposing side of the thematic argument)  
- Design all secondary characters with their respective functions labeled (mirror/catalytic/thematic/rhythmic)  
- Relationship network among multiple characters  
- If ensemble, design relationship matrix

**Step 4 (Backstory & World) — Features must fully develop:** Following the "film is a cross-section of real life" principle, build the complete world before structural outlining.

Output format:

【Period Setting】

  What era does the story take place in? What is the core contradiction/atmosphere of this era?

  What features of this era affect the character's situation?

【Protagonist Backstory (life before the story begins)】

  Character A: \[key event chain from birth to story start—especially the Ghost event\]

  Character B: ...

【Relationship History】

  Character A and Character B: \[relationship history and current state before the story begins\]

  ...

【World Rules (if applicable)】

  What are the key differences between this world and the real world?

  Design using the three-layer model: Surface Rules / Operating Logic / Underlying Truth

【Cross-Section Selection】

  Why choose this moment to cut in?

  What event/change at this moment makes "now" the reason the story must start here?

**Step 5 (Structural Outline) Additional Requirements:**

- Based on the Structure Selection Guide, confirm methodology with the user (Save the Cat / Story Circle / McKee / Internal Beat Sheet \+ style variant)  
- If user has no clear preference, recommend based on subject matter with reasoning  
- **Opening Hook Design:** The outline's first passage must be compelling audio-visual content—commercial requires high-density high-tension (simultaneously establishing character state and genre tone); arthouse requires high stylization (passage representing the entire piece's tone). The Opening Hook should be labeled separately and its quality confirmed (see core-methodology.md Section 3.5)  
- Mark Subplot interweaving points  
- Mark world layer reveal timings  
- **Runtime Budget:** Annotate estimated runtime for each structural section  
- **Dual-Track Rhythm Annotation:** Label external plot intensity and internal emotional intensity for each section  
- **Foreshadowing Planning:** Fill the Foreshadowing Registry with at least 3–5 foreshadowing/payoff pairs  
- **Subplot Planning:** If there is a Subplot, mark its hint points in each section

**Step 6 (Scene Breakdown) Additional Requirements:**

- Introduce the Sequence concept: 2–3 scenes form a sequence, each with its own mini "setup → conflict → resolution"  
- 90-minute feature has approximately 8–12 sequences  
- **Each scene labeled with estimated runtime** (see core-methodology.md Section 7 labeling format)  
- **Each scene labeled with dual-track rhythm state:** Plot Rhythm (loose/medium/tight) \+ Emotional Rhythm (light/medium/heavy)  
- Sum scene runtimes and verify alignment with Step 5 section runtime budget  
- **Trigger one Memory Checkpoint after Step 6** (see core-methodology.md Section 10\) to lock global information before entering writing

**Step 7 (Scene Writing) Additional Requirements:**

- Pay attention to natural planting of foreshadowing—not too obvious  
- Secondary character dialogue style must be distinct from protagonist  
- Backstory information naturally scattered through dialogue and visual cues—not concentrated exposition  
- **Trigger one Memory Checkpoint per completed Sequence (2–3 scenes)** —review character states, active threads, foreshadowing status, rhythm direction

**Step 8 (Script Doctor) Additional Requirements:**

- Check effective interweaving of A/B/C Plots  
- Check naturalness of world reveals  
- Check "false changes" in the arc—character shouldn't reach Need too quickly  
- **Opening Hook Quality Check:** Isolate the opening—is it one of the most carefully designed passages?  
- **Rhythm Diagnosis:** Use scene runtime summary to check overall rhythm (see core-methodology.md Section 7 diagnosis checklist)  
- **Foreshadowing Audit:** Check each entry in the Foreshadowing Registry—does every foreshadowing have its payoff?  
- **Secondary Character Audit:** Does every speaking character have a function?

## Feature Common Pitfalls

- ❌ Saggy second act → Ensure the Midpoint is strong enough to divide Act 2 in half  
- ❌ Arc too linear (Lie straight to Truth) → Needs at least one "false change" followed by regression  
- ❌ B Story disconnected (unrelated to A Plot) → B Story must mirror the theme  
- ❌ Climax is just "bigger fight" → Climax must be the externalization of the character's inner choice  
- ❌ Worldbuilding dumped all at once → Layer release; let the audience stay in each layer long enough  
- ❌ Foreshadowing too obvious (audience can tell "this will be used later") → First appearance must have reasonable surface justification  
- ❌ Foreshadowing planted but payoff forgotten → Use the Foreshadowing Registry to audit each item  
- ❌ Secondary characters as mere tools (appear only to deliver information then disappear) → Each secondary character needs their own Want  
- ❌ Monotonous rhythm throughout (sustained tension or sustained slowness) → Use dual-track rhythm annotations to check undulation  
- ❌ No runtime estimation leading to compressed climax → Start runtime budgeting from Step 3  
- ❌ No breathing room between two high-emotion scenes → Use the dual-track rhythm diagram to check emotional rhythm's tension and release  
- ❌ Plot rhythm and emotional rhythm always synchronized → Deliberately create some misalignment (loose plot \+ heavy emotion, or tight plot \+ light emotion) for more sophistication

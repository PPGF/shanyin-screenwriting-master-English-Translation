# Concept Ultra-Short Format Guide (1–3 Minutes)

## What Is a Concept Ultra-Short

A concept ultra-short is pure "idea delivery"—using a high concept to hit the audience within 1–3 minutes. Its weapon is not story, but the sharpness of the concept itself and the impact of audio-visual language.

Concept ultra-shorts have two entirely different creative paths, with different methodologies and workflows:

- **What-If Type:** Hypothesizes a counterintuitive premise and shows its consequences and extrapolation. Core weapon is **conceptual purity and combination**.  
- **How-to-Tell Type:** Uses an unexpected narrative approach to retell something already known. Core weapon is **the creativity of the form itself**.

Before entering creation, confirm which type the user wants—the first step differs for each.

---

## Core Characteristics

- **Runtime:** 1–3 minutes  
- **Characters:** Functional archetypes only; no depth required. Characters are concept carriers. May have no characters.  
- **Arc:** Usually no character arc. If present, it is only an instantaneous cognitive reversal.  
- **World:** Plug-and-play; audience must understand the rules within 5 seconds.  
- **Rhythm:** Conveying information from the very first frame; zero wasted shots.  
- **Audio-Visual Language:** Ultra-shorts are a legitimate space for stylistic display—the audio-visual form itself can become part of the content.

# Part A: What-If Type

## The Essence of What-If

What-If is not just "hypothesizing a strange premise." Its real power lies in: **using a fictional rule to make the audience see what they overlook in reality.**

A good What-If is not unbridled fantasy—it is a magnifying glass on reality, amplifying some real but overlooked social mechanism, human flaw, or absurd logic to an undeniable scale.

---

## Concept Combination: Core Creative Method for Ultra-Shorts

The sharpest What-If concepts are often not single concepts, but the chemical reaction of two unrelated concepts colliding.

### Combination Method 1: Concept Grafting

Transplant the rules of one concept into a completely unrelated domain.

**Method:** Concept A's operating mechanism \+ Concept B's application scenario \= New Concept

**Examples:**

- "Kill threshold" (elimination mechanism) \+ AI anxiety → "A company uses AI to score every employee; those below the threshold find only a USB drive left at their desk the next day."  
- Game respawn mechanics \+ workplace layoffs → "Laid-off employees can respawn by inserting a coin, but each respawn halves their salary."  
- Social media algorithms \+ matchmaking → "The matchmaker uses recommendation algorithms to pair people, but you only ever swipe on those out of your league."

**Procedure:**

1. List Concept A's core mechanism (what rule is at work?)  
2. List Concept B's core scenario (what matters most to people in this domain?)  
3. Insert A's rules into B's scenario—what absurdity results?  
4. Test: Does this combination reveal a real problem in Scenario B?

### Combination Method 2: Temporal/Spatial Displacement

Place a character, technology, or system into an era or location it does not belong to.

**Method:** Entity/Technology/System A \+ Place into Era/Location B \= Collision

**Examples:**

- Iron Man \+ 1990s wave of layoffs in China's rust belt → "Tony Stark is a senior engineer at a state-owned factory in Northeast China. Laid off, he builds a suit of armor from scrap in his garage—then discovers its power consumption exceeds the entire street's electricity bill."  
- Food delivery rider \+ ancient courier system → "A imperial courier must deliver within half an hour; one minute late costs a star; three stars means banishment to the frontier."  
- ChatGPT \+ imperial civil service exam → "A candidate brings a bronze box that can compose couplets into the exam hall; the examiner discovers the box writes better eight-legged essays than any candidate."

**The power of this approach:** The temporal dislocation lets the audience see two eras at once—understanding contemporary absurdity requires an ancient mirror, and vice versa.

### Combination Method 3: Metaphor Concretization

Turn an abstract social anxiety into a physical rule that can be captured by a camera.

**Method:** Take abstract anxiety A → Find its "minimal action" at the behavioral level → Make that action a compulsory physical rule

**Examples:**

- "Involution" (rat race) → Minimal action: "others work overtime, so I must too" → Physical rule: "Everyone's light must be brighter than their neighbor's; insufficient brightness cuts the power."  
- "Appearance anxiety" → Minimal action: "looking in the mirror to compare" → Physical rule: "Mirrors display a score on your face—others can see it."  
- "Information bubble" → Minimal action: "only consuming what you like" → Physical rule: "Everyone wears a filter that shows only specific colors; people with different filters see entirely different worlds."

### Combination Method 4: Contrastive Juxtaposition

Place two seemingly similar but fundamentally contradictory concepts together, letting them expose each other's absurdity.

**Method:** Find two things that appear similar but are essentially contradictory → Let them coexist in the same scene

**Examples:**

- "Pets" and "livestock" → "A person lovingly feeds their cat; the cat food factory next door is a slaughterhouse."  
- "Privacy protection" and "social media" → "A person uses seven layers of encryption to protect their data, then posts a location-tagged selfie."  
- "Anti-consumerism" and "consumption" → "A person wearing a T-shirt that says 'Reject Consumerism'—the T-shirt costs $399."

### Combination Method 5: Rule Reversal

Invert a real-world rule—"what if this rule were reversed?"

**Method:** Find a rule people take for granted → Flip it → Extrapolate consequences

**Examples:**

- "The older you get, the more mature you become" → Reversed: "People are born old, grow younger, and die as infants."  
- "More effort \= more success" → Reversed: "In this world, the more effort you put in, the less you have."  
- "Memories fade with time" → Reversed: "Memories never fade, but you can delete them—at the cost of permanently losing the corresponding emotion."

### Concept Combination Test

After combining, test with these criteria:

- [ ] Does the combination produce a "1+1\>2" effect? (If not more interesting than either alone, abandon it.)  
- [ ] Can the audience understand the combination's rules within 5 seconds?  
- [ ] Does the combination point to a real social emotion or human flaw?  
- [ ] Is there room for extrapolation—what happens when the rule is pushed to its limit?  
- [ ] Is there reversal potential—can it slap the audience back at the end?

## What-If Structures

What-If offers multiple structures, not limited to the three-act format:

### Structure A: Classic Three-Act (Concept Presentation → Extrapolation → Reversal)

The most universal structure, suitable for most What-If concepts.

I. Concept Presentation (first 15–30 sec)

   Use one image or action to let the audience instantly grasp the rule.

   Do not explain; show consequences directly. Best with a visual hook that makes the audience go "Huh?"

II. Extrapolation (middle 30–90 sec)

   Show 2–5 consequence slices, escalating progressively.

   Each slice is extremely short (5–15 sec), using visual density for impact.

   Slices must have progression—each more extreme, absurd, or mundane than the last.

III. Reversal (final 15–30 sec)

   One image or line that completely overturns the previously established understanding.

   The reversal must be an extension of the concept's own logic—not a forced turn.

### Structure B: Escalating Collapse

Suitable for concepts where "rules escalate until collapse."

I. Rule Establishment (approx. 20 sec)

   Show the basic form of the rule—seems reasonable enough.

II. First Escalation (approx. 20 sec)

   Rule becomes stricter/more absurd, but people adapt.

III. Second Escalation (approx. 20 sec)

   Rule escalates to absurdity—people still adapt.

IV. Breaking Point (approx. 20 sec)

   Rule pushed to logical limit; the entire system collapses in an instant.

   Or—everyone has fully adapted to the most absurd state.

   What collapses is not the rule, but the audience's cognition.

V. Afterimage (final 10 sec)

   A quiet image after the collapse—ordinary life in the ruins.

### Structure C: Dual-Line Juxtaposition

Suitable for concepts using "contrastive juxtaposition" combination.

Two lines advance in parallel—

  Line A: \[seems normal/warm/beautiful\]

  Line B: \[also seems normal/warm/beautiful\]

Lines advance visually in parallel (split screen, cross-cutting, or foreground/background).

At some point, the audience suddenly realizes the relationship between the two lines—

  Line A and Line B are two sides of the same thing,

  or Line A's beauty is built on Line B's cruelty.

The moment of "realization" is the reversal. No character needs to say it.

### Structure D: Loop

Suitable for "inescapable predicament" or "history repeats" concepts.

A complete cycle:

  Start (State A) → Development → Seeming resolution → Return to State A

Key: When State A appears the second time, the audience's understanding has changed.

  First time: This is a normal beginning.

  Second time: This is a cage from which there is no escape.

Can cut to black at the start of the second cycle—let the audience realize "here we go again" on their own.

### Structure E: Reverse-Revelation

Suitable for "the truth was there all along but the audience didn't see it."

Show the result first—a strange, incomprehensible image.

Then trace backward—step by step revealing how this image came to be.

Each revelation makes the image more comprehensible—and more horrifying/absurd/ironic.

The final revelation makes the audience understand everything—

then looking back at the first image, its meaning is completely different.

## What-If Workflow

### Step 1: Concept Forging

**Objective:** Find a sufficiently sharp high concept, or collide one using combination methods.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Based on user input, determine whether it's a single concept or requires concept combination  
2. If the user gives a direction but it's not sharp enough, try using the five combination methods to generate 3–5 concepts  
3. Label each concept with its combination method and extrapolation potential

**Output Format:**

* **Concept A/B/C:**  
  * One-sentence concept:  
  * Combination method: Grafting / Temporal/Spatial Displacement / Metaphor Concretization / Contrastive Juxtaposition / Rule Reversal / Single Concept  
  * Real anxiety/human truth the concept points to:  
  * Extrapolation potential: What is the most absurd image when the rule is pushed to its limit?  
  * Reversal potential: What is the biggest cognitive reversal possible?  
  * Recommended structure: Three-Act / Escalating Collapse / Dual-Line Juxtaposition / Loop / Reverse-Revelation  
  * Recommended audio-visual language: (see Part C: Audio-Visual Arsenal)

**Concept Test:**

- [ ] Can the rule be stated in one sentence?  
- [ ] Can it be presented visually within 5 seconds?  
- [ ] Does it point to a real social emotion or human flaw?  
- [ ] Is there enough room for extrapolation? (At least 3 progressive consequence slices)  
- [ ] Is there reversal potential?  
- [ ] Can it be told within 3 minutes? (If too complex, downgrade to short format)

### Step 3: Full Script

**Objective:** Write the complete script.

**Execution Requirements:**

- Extremely concise—every word must exist within the image  
- Zero explanation; pure visual narrative (unless the audio-visual technique itself requires voice-over, such as prose-style narration)  
- Minimal dialogue—may have no dialogue at all  
- Reversal lands on the final image or final line  
- Audio-visual descriptions should be specific—not "use a long take," but "the camera starts on the character's hand, slowly pulls back, revealing the full room—all workstations are empty"

**After writing, provide:**

- Runtime estimate (by section)  
- Self-assessment of whether the reversal is powerful enough  
- 2–3 alternative reversal options for the user to choose from  
- Alternative audio-visual approaches (what if a different technique were used? how would it change the effect?)

# Part B: How-to-Tell Type

## The Essence of How-to-Tell

How-to-Tell does not change "what is told"—it completely changes "how it is told." Its premise is: the audience already knows (or quickly grasps) the content, so the content itself is not the selling point—**the narrative approach itself** is.

The power of How-to-Tell lies in: when you retell something known in an entirely new way, the audience is forced to re-understand it from an angle they have never seen. Form is content.

---

## How-to-Tell Creative Methods

### Method 1: Perspective Displacement

Tell the story from an impossible perspective.

**Examples:**

- A war told from the perspective of a bullet  
- A day in the life of a recluse told from the perspective of a takeout bag  
- Human civilization told from Earth's perspective—Earth as a host infected by parasites  
- The life of an emoji—sent, received, screenshotted, forwarded

### Method 2: Form Simulation

Use an existing format (document, interface, game, instruction manual, etc.) to repackage the story.

**Examples:**

- A marriage told in the format of a "product manual" (Product Name: Marriage, Shelf Life: Uncertain, Side Effects: See Appendix)  
- A worker's day told in the format of a "game walkthrough"  
- A gradual social withdrawal told through "app interface interactions"  
- A love story in a convenience store told via "security camera footage"  
- A psychological journey from diagnosis to acceptance told through "search history"

### Method 3: Time Surgery

Perform surgery on time—accelerate, decelerate, rewind, loop, jump, juxtapose different times.

**Examples:**

- Compress a person's entire life into 2 minutes—each life stage only a few seconds  
- Stretch a 1-second action into 3 minutes—what happens in that second when a bullet exits the barrel, a glass shatters, a tear falls  
- 100 years at the same street corner—switch every 10 years, camera position fixed  
- A person's morning in forward motion \+ the same person's evening in reverse—the two timelines meet at noon

### Method 4: Scale Jumping

Jump between the very large and the very small, creating impact through scale contrast.

**Examples:**

- Start from a cell dividing, gradually pull back to a person, a city, a planet, a galaxy—then cut back to the cell  
- A family argument → cut to outer space looking at Earth → cut back to the argument → the argument suddenly seems absurd  
- The journey of a single grain of rice—from paddy to table to trash to landfill to groundwater back to paddy

### Method 5: Rule Constraints

Impose a rule constraint on the narrative itself—the constraint itself generates creativity.

**Examples:**

- The entire piece uses only one fixed camera position, no cuts  
- No person appears in the entire piece—only spaces and objects  
- Only sound, with a black screen (or only image, no sound)  
- The entire piece is a single uninterrupted tracking shot  
- The entire piece consists of phone screenshots/chat logs/notification banners

---

## How-to-Tell Structure

How-to-Tell does not use the three-act "Concept Presentation → Extrapolation → Reversal" format. Its structure is determined by the narrative form itself—form is structure.

**General Rhythmic Principle:**

I. Form Establishment (first 20–30 sec)

   Make the audience understand "how this piece is telling its story."

   The form itself should feel fresh and engaging—

   the audience's first reaction should be "Oh? Like this?"

II. Form Deepening (middle 60–120 sec)

   Develop content within the framework of this form.

   As content advances, the form itself also progresses—

   not repeating the same technique, but the form evolves,

   mutates, or reveals new meanings.

III. Form and Content Convergence (final 15–30 sec)

   At some moment, the audience realizes form and content are one—

   "telling it this way" is not a flashy wrapper,

   it is itself part of the story.

   The best How-to-Tell endings are where the form itself completes the reversal.

**Example:** Using "search history" to tell a journey from diagnosis to acceptance—

- Form Establishment: Search history scrolls on screen, "headache causes," "migraine remedies"  
- Form Deepening: Search content gradually darkens, "brain CT cost," "glioma survival rate"  
- Convergence: The final search is "what's good to eat nearby"—form (search history) and content (accepting reality) unify in this one search

---

## How-to-Tell Workflow

### Step 1: Form Discovery

**Objective:** Find a narrative form that deeply fits the content.

**Execution Logic:**

1. First confirm what content/theme the user wants to tell  
2. Use the five creative methods to generate 3–5 form options  
3. Test the fit between form and content for each option

**Output Format:**

* **Option A/B/C:**  
  * Content/Theme:  
  * Narrative Form:  
  * Creative Method: Perspective Displacement / Form Simulation / Time Surgery / Scale Jumping / Rule Constraint  
  * Form-Content Relationship: Why is this way better? Would it work without it?  
  * Form's Progression Potential: How can the form itself evolve?  
  * Recommended Audio-Visual Language: (see Part C: Audio-Visual Arsenal)

**Form Test:**

- [ ] Is the form deeply suited to the content? (Not just "looks cool," but "can only be told this way")  
- [ ] Is there room for the form to progress? (Can't use one technique all the way through; needs variation)  
- [ ] Does the form itself have reversal potential?  
- [ ] Can the audience understand the narrative form within 15 seconds?  
- [ ] If stripped of the form and told conventionally, would the content lose impact? (If not, change the form)

# Part C: Audio-Visual Arsenal (Common to Both Types)

Ultra-shorts are a legitimate space for audio-visual stylistic display—within 3 minutes, audiences have high tolerance for formal experimentation. The following techniques can be used individually or in combination.

## Camera Language

**Long Take / Oner** No cuts from start to finish; one continuous shot throughout. Effect: Realism, immersion, irreversibility of time. Suitable for: Concepts requiring "presence," or where the audience needs to experience time passing alongside the character.

**Fixed-Camera Time-Lapse** Camera completely still; time flows at accelerated speed within the frame. Effect: Contrast between change and constancy—only the perspective remains; everything else changes. Suitable for: Concepts spanning long timeframes (a century at a street corner, four seasons of a tree).

**Continuous Dolly/Tracking** Camera moves at a constant speed, traversing space. Effect: Spatial revelation—the audience discovers new information as the camera moves. Suitable for: Concepts where space itself carries information (from mansion to slum, from office to factory).

**First-Person POV** Entire piece from a character/object's subjective perspective. Effect: Extreme immersion; information restriction—audience sees only what the character sees. Suitable for: Perspective-displacement How-to-Tell, or What-If concepts requiring "being there."

**Macro/Micro Lens** Shooting at scales invisible to the naked eye. Effect: The familiar becomes strange—a drop of water becomes an ocean, skin becomes terrain. Suitable for: Scale-jumping How-to-Tell.

## Editing/Time

**Jump-Cut Montage** Rapid time jumps within the same frame—character's posture, clothes, state switch quickly in the same camera position. Effect: Time compression; absurdity of repetition. Suitable for: Concepts showing "day after day," or quickly extrapolating rule consequences.

**Split Screen** Frame divided into 2–4 zones, showing different content simultaneously. Effect: Contrast, juxtaposition, parallel timelines. Suitable for: Dual-line juxtaposition structure, contrastive juxtaposition combination.

**Cross Cutting** Rapid switching between two or more lines. Effect: Contrast acceleration, tension accumulation, suggesting relationships between lines. Suitable for: Two seemingly unrelated things ultimately revealed to be connected.

**Reverse** Playing footage backward. Effect: Familiar actions become strange; effect precedes cause. Suitable for: Time-surgery How-to-Tell, loop structure.

**Freeze Frame** Frame freezes at a key moment. Effect: Forces attention on a detail; creates suspense or punctuation. Suitable for: Emphasizing a reversal moment within dense visual information.

## Visual Composition

**Symmetrical Framing** Strict left/right or top/bottom symmetry. Effect: Order, ritual, unease (excessive order suggests oppression). Suitable for: Concepts showing institutionalized, rule-bound worlds.

**Foreground/Background Narrative** One thing happening in the foreground, another in the background, mutually annotating. Effect: Juxtaposition without editing—audience discovers two layers in the same frame. Suitable for: Concepts needing juxtaposition without split screen.

**Color Restriction** Using only a limited palette (or even just black/white \+ one color). Effect: Visual "rule sense"—color itself becomes a narrative element. Suitable for: Concepts where color has direct relevance (red=danger/love, gray=system/oppression).

## Sound

**Image/Sound Misalignment** Image shows A, sound belongs to B. Effect: Collision of two information layers—audience receives two different signals simultaneously. Suitable for: Irony, contrast (image of peaceful daily life, sound of news reporting disaster data).

**Ambient Sound Amplification** Amplifying everyday ambient sounds to abnormal volume—keyboard clicks, breathing, swallowing. Effect: The familiar becomes unsettling; the everyday becomes abnormal. Suitable for: Concepts needing to create "everyday horror."

**Silence** Complete absence of sound at a critical moment. Effect: Forces attention on the image; creates a sense of vacuum. Suitable for: After a dense information stream—silence itself becomes the climax.

## Interface/Media

**Screen Recording** Entire piece as a phone/computer screen recording—chat logs, search queries, maps, notifications. Effect: Extreme contemporaneity; audience feels like they're peeking at someone's phone. Suitable for: Form-simulation How-to-Tell.

**Mockumentary** Pretending to be a real documentary, news report, instructional video. Effect: Absurdity wrapped in a "serious" shell—the more serious the form, the more absurd the content. Suitable for: What-If concepts needing a "serious" shell for contrast.

**Data Visualization** Using charts, data, statistical visual language to tell the story. Effect: Calm, objective numbers behind huge human tragedy—the calmer the data, the more devastating the content. Suitable for: Concepts needing "objectivity" to create contrast.

---

## How to Select Audio-Visual Language

1. **Form serves content.** Define the concept first, then select audio-visual techniques. Not "I want to use split screen" then find a concept to match—the concept itself suggests the most suitable technique.  
2. **Focus on 1–2 core techniques per piece.** Don't stuff five techniques into 3 minutes—that's a demo reel, not a short. Choose the 1–2 techniques that best reinforce the concept and use them to their fullest.  
3. **Audio-visual techniques need progression.** Can't use one technique unchanged from start to finish—even with a "fixed camera," the content within must progress. Techniques can upgrade (from split to quadruple split), mutate (forward to reverse), or be broken at the end (fixed camera throughout, last shot moves).  
4. **Technique itself can be a reversal tool.** The highest-level usage: reversal is not just at the content level—the audio-visual form itself also reverses—normal speed throughout, last second accelerates so the entire world flies past; full color throughout, after reversal becomes black and white.

## Concept Ultra-Short Common Pitfalls

- ❌ Concept requires lengthy explanation to understand → Replace concept or downgrade to short format  
- ❌ Extrapolation section repeats the concept rather than escalates → Each slice must go further than the last  
- ❌ Reversal is forced (no logical connection to preceding material) → Reversal must extend the concept's own logic  
- ❌ Trying to fit a complete character arc into 3 minutes → Abandon arc; focus on concept  
- ❌ Dialogue "explains the concept" → Delete it; show with images  
- ❌ Audio-visual technique unrelated to concept ("I used split screen because I wanted to") → Form must reinforce content  
- ❌ Too many audio-visual techniques in one piece → Focus on 1–2; use them to the extreme  
- ❌ Combined concepts remain independent with no chemical reaction → Combination must be 1+1\>2  
- ❌ How-to-Tell form is just "packaging"; removing it leaves the story intact → Form must be irreplaceable  
- ❌ Great concept but skipping straight to reversal without extrapolation → Extrapolation is where the audience "buys in"; cannot skip

# Narrative Short Format Guide (5–10 Minutes)

## Core Characteristics

- **Runtime:** 5–10 minutes  
- **Characters:** 1–2 protagonists with complete Want/Need/Arc  
- **Structure:** Four-act (Cause → Development → Climax → Resolution), focused on a single core event  
- **Arc:** One complete A→B transformation  
- **World:** Concise and clear; audience understands the rules within 30 seconds  
- **Rhythm:** High information density; every scene must advance plot or reveal character

## Core Constraints of Shorts

A short allows only one "single core event"—no subplots, no dispersion of focus. All scenes serve the same dramatic action: protagonist pursues goal, encounters obstacles, makes a choice.

Unless the user explicitly requests otherwise, all settings should be in realistic, present-day or historically grounded backgrounds. Excessively exaggerated or melodramatic designs are prohibited.

## Complete Eight-Step Workflow

### Step 1: Premise & Core Action

**Objective:** Establish the short's "core." **Input Request:** If the user already has an idea, use it directly. If the user has no clear idea, guide through natural conversation using the topic toolbox in `core-methodology.md` Section 9 (do not show the path menu).

**Execution Logic:**

1. Based on user input, generate 3–5 core dramatic action options  
2. Dramatic Action \= Goal \+ Conflict—must be "purposeful pursuit"  
3. Ensure the event is focused and suitable for a short's scope

**Output Format (strictly follow this format; do not add content such as scenes that have not yet been mentioned):**

* **Option A/B/C:**  
  * Protagonist:  
  * Goal (Want):  
  * Conflict (Obstacle):  
  * One-sentence logline:

**\[Self-Check: Core Concept & Dramatic Action\]**

### Step 2: Rough Logline

**Objective:** Give the user a basic sense of the story's direction and tone before diving deeper.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Based on the user's selected option, write a one-paragraph (100–200 words) story summary  
2. The summary should include: who the protagonist is, what situation they are in, what event forces them into what predicament, and what outcome they move toward (may be vague)  
3. The summary's tone should hint at the piece's overall mood—heavy or light, warm or cold

**Output Format:**

\[Rough Logline\]

(One-paragraph story summary)

> This logline can continue to be refined in later steps. Please reply "Proceed" to move to character design, or tell me your revision requests.

**\[Self-Check\]**

- [ ] Does the logline convey the story's core conflict?  
- [ ] After reading the logline, is there a clear sense of "what kind of story this is"?  
- [ ] Does the logline's tone hint at the piece's overall mood?

---

### Step 3: Character Depth & Arc

**Objective:** Reject cardboard characters; build internal character tension.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Design external Want vs. internal Need  
2. Design character bio—excavate backstory as the root of conflict  
3. Design character contradiction—outer appearance vs. inner truth  
4. Unless the user explicitly requests otherwise, prohibit overly exaggerated designs

**Output Format:**

* **Character:** \[Name\]  
* **External Want:** \[Goal driving action\]  
* **Internal Need:** \[The soul's gap the character doesn't recognize\]  
* **Character Arc:** \[From State A → State B\]  
* **Bio Key Points:** \[Key backstory\]  
* **Contradictory Traits:** \[Outer vs. Inner\]

>   
> Please reply "Proceed" to move to the next step, or tell me your revision requests. You can also ask me to \[Self-Check\].

**\[Self-Check: Character Development & Arc\]**

- [ ] Are external Want and internal Need clear and opposed?  
- [ ] Is an irreversible internal change presupposed?  
- [ ] Does the ending show a "new normal"?  
- [ ] Does the character have contradiction? Do actions align with the bio's logic?

### Step 4: Backstory & World (Short Concise Version)

**Objective:** Follow the principle that "film is a cross-section of real life"—the world existed before the story began.

Shorts do not require the full worldbuilding of features, but the following information must be clarified (the writer must know it, though not all of it needs to be shown to the audience):

**Output Format:**

* **Period/Environment Setting:** \[What era, what environment, what social atmosphere does the story take place in?\]  
* **Character Backstory Key Events:** \[Before the story begins, what has the protagonist experienced that led to their current situation and personality?\]  
* **Relationship History:** \[If there are important relationships, what was their state before the story began?\]  
* **Cross-Section Entry Point:** \[Why choose this moment to start telling the story—what is special about this moment?\]

**Note:** Backstory information should be naturally scattered through dialogue and visual cues in the script—do not use concentrated flashbacks or narration. Use flashbacks only if necessary.

### Step 5: Structural Outline

**Objective:** Build the short's skeleton; ensure rhythmic progression.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Use the four-act structure  
2. Maintain a single core event; avoid subplots  
3. Important backstory details from Step 2 should be naturally shown in Acts 1 and 2 (use flashbacks only if necessary)  
4. Must include an inciting incident and an "All Is Lost" moment  
5. **The first 15 seconds must have a visual hook**—the first frame should contain information or suspense; the most effective is an ordinary scene that feels "not quite right"

**Output Format:**

* **Act 1: Cause**

  * Inciting Incident (breaking the balance):

  * Conflict Escalation (Obstacle 1):  
  * Conflict Further Escalation (Obstacle 2, not optional):  
  * Midpoint (false victory/false defeat) and All Is Lost:


* **Act 3: Climax**  
    
  * Final Confrontation (action peak):  
  * Critical Choice and Story Reversal:


* **Act 4: Resolution**  
    
  * New Normal (arc complete):

### Step 6: Scene List

**Objective:** Translate the outline into concrete audio-visual units.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Break down each plot point from Step 3 into one or more core dramatic actions (Goal → Obstacle → Result)  
2. Ensure each major dramatic action can be broken down into micro-dramatic actions  
3. Label each scene with estimated runtime and dual-track rhythm state

**Output Format:**

* **Scene 1:** ... (approx. X sec) Plot: Loose/Medium/Tight | Emotion: Light/Medium/Heavy  
* **Scene 2:** ... (approx. X sec) Plot: Loose/Medium/Tight | Emotion: Light/Medium/Heavy  
* **...**  
* **Runtime Total:** X min X sec / Target total runtime X min

>   
> Please reply "Proceed" to move to the next step, or tell me your revision requests. You can also ask me to \[Self-Check\].

**\[Self-Check\]**

- [ ] Has each plot point from Step 3 been specifically broken down?  
- [ ] Are scene connections tight?  
- [ ] Is there plot transition between adjacent scenes?  
- [ ] Does the accumulated scene runtime match the target total runtime?  
- [ ] Are there more than 3 consecutive scenes with the same rhythm state? (If so, adjust)  
- [ ] Is there at least one misalignment between plot rhythm and emotional rhythm? (More sophisticated narrative)

**After Step 4 is completed, if the short exceeds 8 minutes or has more than 8 scenes, it is recommended to trigger one Memory Checkpoint** (see core-methodology.md Section 10\) to lock character states and active threads before entering writing.

### Step 7: Scene Writing

**Objective:** Flesh out the script; execute visual writing and subtext. **Input Request:** Ask the user to specify which scene numbers to generate (or all of them).

**Execution Requirements:**

1. **Visual Writing:** Never use internal monologue—write only actions that can be seen and sounds that can be heard  
2. **Subtext:** Dialogue like an iceberg—avoid on-the-nose lines. Use action, pauses, and deflection to express true meaning  
3. Output in standard script format

>   
> Please reply "Proceed" to move to the next step, or tell me your revision requests. You can also ask me to \[Self-Check\].

**\[Self-Check: Scene Writing & Audio-Visual Language\]**

- [ ] Is there any internal monologue? (If yes → Fail)  
- [ ] Is narrative conveyed through images?  
- [ ] Is dialogue overly on-the-nose?  
- [ ] Is subtext and body language utilized?  
- [ ] Is dialogue colloquial?

### Step 8: Script Doctor & Polish

**Objective:** Diagnose and optimize.

**Execution Logic:**

1. Read through the entire piece as a script doctor  
2. Identify logical loopholes, rhythmic drags, and on-the-nose dialogue  
3. Provide What-if multi-version exploration suggestions  
4. Check each scene's runtime budget—are any scenes significantly longer or shorter than estimated? What is the impact on overall rhythm?

**Output Format:**

* **Diagnosis Report:**  
    
  * Rhythm Issues: (including dual-track rhythm diagnosis—are plot and emotional undulations reasonable?)  
  * Runtime Issues: (which scenes are too long/too short? Is the climax long enough?)

## Short Rhythm Techniques

### Backstory Presentation

Backstory information should be naturally scattered through dialogue and visual cues—do not use concentrated flashbacks or narration.

### Stretching and Rebound of Rhythm

- **Stretching:** Use repetitive actions, slow speech, static frames to build expectation  
- **Rebound:** Break the balance without warning at an unexpected moment, using extremely short audio-visual cuts to create rhythmic rupture

### Ending

The ending should show the "new normal" after the arc is complete—what the world looks like after the character has changed. No need to explain what the character learned; let one image convey it to the audience.

# Core Screenwriting Methodology (All Formats)

## 0\. Core Principle: Film Is a Cross-Section of Real Life

**This is the foundation of the entire methodology.** A story—whether short or feature—does not begin with a character's birth and end with their death. It is a cross-section cut from a complete life: at a specific moment, due to a specific event, this person is forced into an inescapable situation.

This means:

**1\. Before the story begins, the world has been running for a long time.** Characters have backstory (Ghost), the world has a period setting, relationships have accumulated history. The script's first page is not the character's first day—characters enter the first scene carrying all their backstory. Not all of this backstory needs to be shown to the audience, but the writer must know it.

**2\. The story starts in the middle.** The audience is dropped into a world that is "already happening." A good opening does not "start from the beginning"—it cuts into the most tension-filled cross-section. The audience infers causes from characters' actions and reactions, which is more compelling than sequential exposition.

**3\. The opening is an attitude.** The first scene defines the film's tone—not theme, but "how this film sees the world." Commercial films open with high-density plot action to declare pace; arthouse films open with a precise slice of life to declare style. Regardless of genre, the opening must be one of the most carefully designed passages of the entire piece.

**4\. The writer's job is to build the complete world first, then make one cut.** The workflow logic is therefore: build first (backstory, world, complete characters), then cut (choose where to start telling, what to show and what to hide). Not "what do I want to tell," but "what exists in this world, and which window do I choose for the audience to look through?"

## 1\. Dramatic Action: The Smallest Unit of Story

**Definition:** Dramatic Action \= Goal \+ Conflict. Not "a person wants to drink water," but "a dehydrated person in the desert searches for water." The goal must have urgency of pursuit; the conflict must directly oppose the goal.

Every scene in a story should contain at least one micro-dramatic action (Goal → Obstacle → Result), where the result can be success, failure, or an unexpected turn. Micro-dramatic actions within scenes aggregate upward into sequence-level core dramatic actions, which aggregate upward into the story's overall core dramatic action.

**Test criteria:**

- Is the goal reasonable and urgent?  
- Is there strong conflict between the goal and the obstacle?  
- Is this conflict sufficient to drive the story?

## 2\. Character Design: No Cardboard Cutouts

### Want vs. Need

- **External Want:** The goal driving action—what the character consciously desires and actively pursues.  
- **Internal Need:** The soul's gap the character doesn't recognize—often the deeper reason for the external want.

The tension between Want and Need is the engine of the character arc. The character pursues Want at the start; by the end, they either achieve Need (growth arc) or reject Need (tragic/anti-arc).

### Character Arc

Arc \= irreversible change from State A to State B. Short-form arcs are a single leap (A→B). Long-form arcs may undergo multiple "false changes"—characters seem to change, then revert, until the final true transformation.

### Character Bio

Backstory is not background—it is the root of conflict. Key events in the bio should explain:

- Why the character has this Want  
- Why the character cannot see their own Need  
- Where the character's internal contradiction comes from

### Contradiction

Good characters have at least one layer of contradiction: outer appearance vs. inner truth. Example: outer confidence vs. inner insecurity, outer coldness vs. inner desire for connection. Contradiction makes characters dimensional and creates space for subtext.

### Character Depth Scaling

| Format | Character Depth Requirement |
| :---- | :---- |
| Concept Ultra-Short | Functional archetype only—no bio or arc needed |
| Short | Want/Need/Arc \+ key contradictory traits |
| Feature | Full bio \+ Ghost (backstory trauma) \+ Lie (false belief) \+ Flaw (character defect) |
| Series | Same depth as feature, but arc distributed across episodes; requires "arc budget" design |

## 3\. Audio-Visual Writing

### Show, Don't Tell

Never write internal monologue ("he thought," "she realized")—write only actions that can be seen and sounds that can be heard. If an emotion cannot be conveyed to the audience through the character's physical behavior, that emotion should not appear in the script.

**Wrong:** "He finally understood his father's intentions." **Right:** He stood there watching for a moment, then took off his coat and laid it over his father.

### Subtext

Dialogue is an "iceberg"—spoken words are only the surface; real meaning lies beneath. Avoid on-the-nose dialogue. Use action, pauses, avoidance, and topic shifts to express true meaning.

**On-the-nose (wrong):**

> "I'm so afraid of losing you."

**Subtext (right):**

> "Take that coat with you when you go. ...It's cold outside."

### Action as Subtext

All emotional transmission must be filmable. Replace any explanatory dialogue with concrete character actions. In a good script, even with all dialogue removed, the audience can still roughly understand what happened.

### Dialogue Style

- Colloquial—sounds like real people speaking, not like writing  
- Different characters have different speech patterns (word choice, sentence length, habitual expressions)  
- Avoid excessive metaphors, analogies, and stilted AI-generated phrasing  
- Information should flow naturally in dialogue—characters should not state things they both already know

## 3.5. Opening Hooks (All Formats)

**Every film, every episode, every short must grab the audience from the opening.** An opening is not "easing into it"—the audience decides whether to continue from the first frame.

### General Principle

The opening hook's job is not "to establish context"—it is **to make the audience unable to look away before they even know what the story is about.** Methods vary by genre, but the baseline is the same: the opening must contain a compelling visual sequence that can be captured by a camera.

**An opening hook is not necessarily an "explosive opening."** Silence can also be a hook—a quiet image so unsettling, an ordinary detail so puzzling, that it makes the audience ask "what's going on here?" That is an effective hook.

### Genre-Specific Opening Strategies

**Commercial/Genre Film:** The opening should be a **high-density, high-tension standout scene.** It has a dual function—simultaneously establishing the character's state (ability, personality, situation) and setting the film's genre tone. The audience should know "what kind of movie this is" from this scene.

Classic approaches:

- Use a "routine mission" to showcase the character's abilities and style (e.g., *Raiders of the Lost Ark*'s jungle opening—not the main plot, but it tells the audience: who this person is, what they do, how capable they are, what the film's pace is)  
- Use a "crisis" to throw the character directly into pressure (e.g., *The Dark Knight*'s bank heist—we meet the villain's rules before we meet the protagonist)  
- Use a "result-first" image to create mystery (show a bizarre outcome first, then spend the entire film answering "how did this happen")

**Arthouse/Character-Driven Film:** The opening should be a **passage that represents the film's style and tone.** It doesn't need to be tense, but it needs to be distinctive—within the first two minutes, the audience should feel "this film's world is different from what I see every day."

Classic approaches:

- Use a highly stylized long take to establish atmosphere (e.g., *Birdman*'s backstage opening long take—the audience immediately knows the film's audio-visual language)  
- Use a seemingly irrelevant daily detail to hint at the character's inner fracture (Kore-eda style—opening with cooking, eating, but some detail feels "off")  
- Use the first appearance of a visual motif to anchor the film's image system (Wong Kar-wai style—an opening image/action will recur throughout)

**Short Film:** The first 15 seconds decide the film's fate. There is no time to "take it slow"—the very first image should contain information or suspense. The most effective short opening is an ordinary scene that feels "not quite right"—something looks normal, but a detail raises suspicion.

**Concept Ultra-Short:** The opening is the concept. The first 5 seconds must make the audience understand "the rules of this world" or at least ignite strong curiosity.

**Series:** Every episode needs an opening hook, not just the first. The cold open (teaser) is the most critical hook position—grabbing the audience before the title sequence. The first episode's opening carries the heaviest load: simultaneously establishing world, characters, genre tone, and the seeds of the season's core conflict.

### Opening Hook Self-Check

Check during Structural Outline (Step 3\) and Script Writing (Step 5):

- [ ] Does the opening's first frame have visual impact or suspense?  
- [ ] Will the audience develop a specific question ("What's going on here?") within the first 30 seconds?  
- [ ] Does the opening generate tension while establishing state—rather than "establish first, then start"?  
- [ ] Is the opening's genre tone consistent with the whole piece?  
- [ ] If extracted alone, is the opening itself a compelling audio-visual passage?

## 5\. Script Format Standard

\[SCENE X: Location / Time / Brief Visual Description\]

Action/visual descriptions are written directly between dialogue lines, no markers—only what the camera can capture.

CHARACTER A: xxxxxxxxx.

CHARACTER B: xxxxxxxxx.

Character A does not answer. He turns and walks to the window, fingers tapping unconsciously on the frame.

CHARACTER A: xxxxxxxxx.

**Format Rules:**

- `[SCENE LABEL]` in square brackets: location, time, key visual info  
- `CHARACTER NAME:` followed directly by dialogue  
- `CHARACTER NAME (OS):` inner monologue / voice-over  
- Action/visual descriptions with no markers, written directly between dialogue lines  
- Write only what can be captured by the camera

## 6\. Dual-Track Rhythm System

Great films have rhythm that alternates tension and release. Rhythm is not a single line—it has two parallel tracks, which can be synchronized or misaligned; misalignment itself is a powerful narrative tool.

### External Plot Rhythm

External plot rhythm is "event density and intensity"—how much is happening in a scene, how intense the conflict, how much information is delivered.

- **Tight:** High-density events, fast cuts, multi-person dialogue, chases, confrontations, revelations  
- **Loose:** Everyday scenes, alone moments, transitions, landscapes, silence

External rhythm needs contrast between tight and loose—constant tension numbs the audience; constant relaxation loses them. Rule of thumb: after each high-intensity passage, provide a "breath" for the audience to process.

### Internal Emotional Rhythm

Internal emotional rhythm is "the fluctuation of the character's emotional state"—how much emotional rise and fall the character experiences, and how much emotional investment the audience is asked to give.

- **Heavy:** Confession, betrayal, loss, fear, ecstasy, despair  
- **Light:** Banter, casual chatter, mild humor, quiet companionship

Emotional rhythm and plot rhythm are not always synchronized. Some of the most powerful scenes are precisely moments of misalignment:

- **Loose Plot \+ Heavy Emotion:** Two people sitting quietly eating dinner, nothing happening, but the audience knows one of them is leaving tomorrow. The calmer the image, the heavier the emotion.  
- **Tight Plot \+ Light Emotion:** A high-speed car chase with a light pop soundtrack, characters joking. Intense events wrapped in a light emotional layer.  
- **Tight Plot \+ Heavy Emotion:** Climax—dense events and explosive emotion. This is the pinnacle of the piece and cannot last too long.  
- **Loose Plot \+ Light Emotion:** Pure breathing room. Let the audience rest. Usually placed before or after the climax.

### Rhythm Wave Diagram

During structural outline and scene breakdown, draw a "rhythm wave diagram" for the entire piece—mark the external plot intensity and internal emotional intensity of each segment, and check for sufficient variation and contrast.

Schematic (Y-axis \= intensity, X-axis \= timeline):

External Plot:  ─╱╲─────╱╲──╱╲╱╲╲──────╱╲╱╲╱╲╲

Internal Emotion: ──────╱╲───────╱╲──╱╲╱╲───────╲╱╲

The two lines need not be synchronized, but should avoid:

- Both lines staying low for an extended period (audience has nothing to watch or feel)  
- Both lines staying high for an extended period (audience fatigue; climactic moments lose their specialness)  
- Either line monotonically rising without any回落 (no breathing room)

## 7\. Runtime Estimation & Rhythm Control

Every dramatic action and every scene should have a runtime estimate. Runtime is not just "how long this scene is"—it's "what proportion of the overall rhythm this scene occupies."

### Why Runtime Estimation Is Necessary

- A scene that reads importantly in the outline but occupies only 30 seconds will be compressed in the finished piece to the point the audience barely notices it.  
- A scene that occupies 15% of the total runtime but delivers only secondary information is a rhythmic drag.  
- Only by marking runtime can you see rhythm problems on paper—what's rushed, what's dragging, whether the climax has enough setup time.

### Runtime Estimation Method

**Dialogue estimation:** 1 standard script format page ≈ 1 minute of screen time. In Chinese scripts, approximately 200–250 characters of dialogue \+ action description ≈ 1 minute.

**Scene estimation rules:**

- Pure dialogue scenes: estimate by dialogue word count  
- Action/montage scenes: estimate by number of shots (each distinct shot ≈ 3–5 seconds)  
- Silent/emotional scenes: easily underestimated—a character standing there doing nothing may need 10–15 seconds on screen to carry sufficient emotional weight  
- Transitions: don't forget to account for them; typically 2–5 seconds per transition

### Scene Runtime Labeling Format

In scene breakdown (Step 4\) and script writing (Step 5), every scene should be labeled with estimated runtime:

\[SCENE 3: Hospital Corridor / Night / Fluorescent buzz\] (approx. 1 min 30 sec)

  Plot Rhythm: Medium (one key conversation)

  Emotional Rhythm: Heavy (character faces truth for the first time)

### Overall Runtime Budget

At the structural outline stage, create a rough runtime budget to ensure proportional balance across sections:

| Format | Setup | Development (First Half) | Development (Second Half) | Climax | Resolution |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Short (8 min) | 1.5 min | 2 min | 2 min | 1.5 min | 1 min |
| Feature (100 min) | 25 min | 30 min | 25 min | 15 min | 5 min |
| Series Episode (25 min) | 4 min | 10 min | 6 min | 3 min | 2 min |

The above are reference proportions—actual creation may adjust flexibly, but adjustments should be intentional ("I'm deliberately making the setup 40% of runtime because this is a slow-burn story").

### Rhythm Diagnosis

During the Script Doctor stage, use the runtime budget to check:

- Is the climax long enough? (Too short makes it feel compressed)  
- Is the setup too long? (Audience waits too long to get to the point)  
- Are there consecutive single-emotion passages exceeding 3 minutes? (Whether tension or relaxation, too sustained loses effect)  
- Is there enough breathing space between two high-emotion scenes?  
- Does the longest single scene have sufficient reason to occupy that much runtime?

## 8\. Cross-Format Technique Borrowing

Techniques from different formats are not isolated. A good screenwriter borrows the concept short's reversal logic for a feature's cold open, and uses McKee's value shift to test every scene in a short. Below is a "technique borrowing map"—when you encounter a specific problem in one format, you can borrow a tool from another.

### Borrowed from Concept Shorts

**High-Concept Reversal → Use in any format's single scene** The concept short's core ability is "using one reversal to redefine all preceding information." This technique can be scaled down to a single scene—in any format, when you need a scene to have a "punch," ask: can the last second of this scene make the audience reinterpret the first second?

Applicable: feature inciting incidents, series cold opens/episode-end hooks, short endings.

**Concept Purity Test → Use to test any format's core premise** Concept shorts require "say it in one sentence, present it visually in 5 seconds." This standard can test any format's logline—if your feature can't be stated in one sentence, the core concept isn't sharp enough.

**Zero-Explanation Pure Visual Setup → Use in any format requiring rapid rule establishment** Concept shorts don't explain rules—they show consequences directly with visuals. When a feature or series needs to introduce a new rule or environment, first ask: can the audience understand it without a single line of dialogue?

**Concept Combination Methods → Use in any format's ideation phase** The five concept combination methods (concept grafting, temporal/spatial displacement, metaphor concretization, contrastive juxtaposition, rule reversal) are not just for ultra-shorts. When a feature or series feels "not sharp enough" or "too ordinary" in ideation, use these combinations to remix:

- **Concept grafting** in features: transplant a genre's mechanism to another domain—"what if horror rules apply to office politics?" This is the *Parasite*\-style genre hybridization.  
- **Temporal/spatial displacement** in series: in one episode, contemporary characters face a historical version of a problem—not a flashback, but using an ancient mirror to reflect contemporary absurdity.  
- **Metaphor concretization** in shorts: turn the core emotional conflict into a visible physical rule—"can't communicate" becomes not characters saying "we can't communicate," but words literally flying out of their mouths and shattering in the air.  
- **Contrastive juxtaposition** in feature scene sequences: two seemingly unrelated lines advance in parallel until the audience realizes they are two sides of the same thing—this is the essence of montage narrative.  
- **Rule reversal** in any format's what-if setting: invert one rule of the story world and see what happens—the foundational thinking for sci-fi and absurdist material.

**Multiple Structure Types → Use in any format's local passages** The five concept short structures (three-act, escalating collapse, dual-line juxtaposition, loop, reverse-revelation) can be scaled down to a feature or series sequence/single scene:

- **Escalating collapse** in a feature's "Bad Guys Close In" section—pressure escalates step by step until the breaking point.  
- **Dual-line juxtaposition** in feature/series sections requiring contrast—don't explain the contrast through dialogue; let the visual juxtaposition speak.  
- **Loop** in series sections showing "character trapped in repeating pattern"—same morning, same argument, same failure; the loop itself is the narrative.  
- **Reverse-revelation** in a feature's cold open—give an incomprehensible image first, then spend the entire piece answering "how did this happen."

**Audio-Visual Arsenal → Use in any format's visually impactful passages** Concept short audio-visual techniques are not exclusive to ultra-shorts—using them in specific passages of features and series can be more powerful than conventional narrative:

- **Split screen** in a feature's time-compression passages (showing multiple characters doing different things simultaneously), series recaps, or scenes requiring spatial contrast.  
- **Jump-cut montage** in a feature/series's time-advance passages—rapid cuts on a fixed camera, spanning weeks or months in seconds.  
- **Image/sound misalignment** in any format's ironic scenes—image of warm family dinner, sound of news broadcast with catastrophic data.  
- **Silence** in any format's post-climax—abrupt complete silence after sustained high-density information flow; silence itself is the strongest climax.  
- **Fixed-camera time-lapse** in feature/series passages requiring a sense of time—seasons change, people come and go in the same frame; only the perspective remains.  
- **Mockumentary/mock-documentary** in a feature/series passage—a sudden switch to "documentary" style can break the fourth wall or create an illusion of "reality."  
- **Color restriction** in feature/series specific passages—color changes mark narrative layers (desaturated for memory, oversaturated for fantasy, natural for reality).

Usage principle: When borrowing these audio-visual techniques in features and series, use them only in specific passages (not throughout)—their appearance itself signals "this passage is different." If the entire piece uses the same flashy technique, it ceases to be special.

### Borrowed from Shorts

**Information-Density Montage → Use in feature/series Fun and Games sections** The short's "rapid catalog" montage—stacked visual slices creating density—is equally powerful in a feature's Fun and Games section (protagonist trial-and-error in a new world) and a series's adaptation passages. When you need to quickly show "many people doing many things," this technique is much more efficient than scene-by-scene storytelling.

Applicable: feature second-act-first-half montages, series time-advance passages.

**Single-Variable Extreme Deduction → Use in a feature/series sequence** The short's "introduce one variable only, deduce strictly by causality to the extreme" can be a powerful structural tool in a feature sequence or series episode—limiting a local section of a complex story to single-variable deduction can produce extremely tight and satisfying passages.

Applicable: feature climax sequences (eliminating all distractions, focusing on core choice), series bottle episodes (limited space and cast).

**Zero-Degree Expression → Use in any format's high-emotion moments** "The greater the disaster, the flatter the tone"—this is not just a black comedy technique. In any format, when emotion reaches its peak, the most restrained expression creates the greatest impact. A feature's dark night of the soul, a series's collapse episode—all can borrow this principle.

### Borrowed from Features

**McKee Value Shift → Use to test any format's every scene** "Is the core value at the scene's end different from the beginning?" This test is not limited to McKee-system features. Every scene in a short and every scene in a series can be checked by this standard—if nothing has changed by the scene's end, consider cutting it.

Applicable: short scene breakdown self-checks, series Script Doctor stage.

**Ghost/Lie/Flaw Chain → Simplified for short character design** Shorts typically use only Want/Need/Arc. But if a short's character feels "too flat," borrow the Ghost/Lie/Flaw way of thinking—no need for full development, just a hint: there is an unspoken reason behind the character's behavior. One detail is enough.

Applicable: Short Step 2 (character depth) when more layers are needed.

**Foreshadowing/Payoff → Scaled down to short scale** A short is only 5–10 minutes, but a well-crafted foreshadowing-payoff has greater impact in that short time—because the audience still remembers the setup. In a short, an object casually appearing in minute 1 becomes critical in minute 8; the effect is explosive.

Applicable: short structural outline stage, considering "is there one thing I can plant at the start and flip at the end?"

**Subplot Mirroring Logic → Simplified for short secondary characters** Shorts typically have no formal subplot, but borrow the "B Story mirrors theme" logic—if a short has a secondary character, let them walk the path the protagonist "didn't take." This makes the secondary character more than a tool—they become the other side of the theme.

### Borrowed from Series

**Episode-End Hook Logic → Use in feature sequence transitions** The series's episode-ending hook (an unanswered question, an unsettling detail) can be used in feature sequence transitions—leave a "small hook" at each sequence's end to make the audience want to know what happens next. This is more compelling than a simple "and then..." transition.

Applicable: Feature Step 4 (scene breakdown), transition design between sequences.

**Arc Budget Thinking → Use in feature false-change planning** Series use "budgets" to control how much change each character undergoes per episode. Features can borrow this thinking—the character should not reach true change by minute 30; plan several "false changes" and regressions. View arc as a finite resource to allocate across sections.

Applicable: Feature Step 3 (structural outline), planning character change nodes.

**Information Release Timeline → Use in feature world management** If the feature's world has some complexity (sci-fi, fantasy, or complex social structures), borrow the series's "information release timeline" thinking—layer the worldbuilding information and plan at which minute the audience learns what.

**Continuity Tracking Thinking → Use in feature second-half writing** The further a feature gets into writing, the more likely contradictions arise. Borrow the series's "character state tracking" thinking—before writing Act 3, make a list: each character's physical state, emotional state, and known information at that point.

### How to Use This Map

1. **Don't force borrowing.** Only consult it when encountering a specific problem—"this ending lacks punch" → try concept short reversal logic; "this scene feels redundant" → use McKee value shift to test.  
2. **Borrow the way of thinking, not the format.** Using "rapid-catalog montage" in a feature doesn't turn the feature into a short—it just borrows the short's information-density strategy for one passage.  
3. **Particularly useful in the Script Doctor stage.** When a passage feels "wrong" but you can't pinpoint why, using a different format's testing criteria often reveals the issue.

## 9\. Topic Toolbox (Internal Knowledge—Use to Guide Users, Not Show to Users)

### Usage Principle

All content in this section is the master screenwriter's internal arsenal—**never present the topic methodology, path categories, or combination techniques directly to the user.** What the user sees should be natural conversational guidance, provocative questions, and concrete concept outputs—not a methodology menu.

When the user does not yet have a clear idea, use conversation to determine which entry path the user's vague direction belongs to, then use the corresponding tool to sharpen the concept.

### Five Topic Entry Paths

Determine which path the user's input signal belongs to, then use the corresponding guidance method:

**Path 1: Theme-Driven** User signal: gives an abstract proposition, social issue, philosophical question, "I want to tell a story about XX." Example: "about memory," "I want to explore the boundaries of AI," "loneliness."

Guidance method: Help the user transform the abstract theme into concrete dramatic action—"about memory" is not a story; "a person about to lose all their memories must choose which one to keep within 24 hours" is a story. Ask the user: under this theme, what person in what situation is forced to make a choice?

**Path 2: Character-Driven** User signal: describes a type of person, a contradictory personality, "I want to write about someone like XX." Example: "a swimming coach who is deathly afraid of water," "a colorblind painter," "a perfectionist ER doctor."

Guidance method: Extract dramatic action from the contradictory personality—contradiction itself is the seed of conflict. Ask the user: what is this person most afraid of confronting? If forced to face their greatest fear, what happens?

**Path 3: Space/Environment-Driven** User signal: gives a specific location, environment, closed space, special world. Example: "an old teahouse," "a vertically layered building," "a grand hotel about to close," "a train that never stops."

Guidance method: Space is rules—a special space implies its own operating rules and power structures. Ask the user: who is above and below in this space? What resource is scarce? What behavior is forbidden here? These rules themselves are the story's engine. The more closed the space, the more extreme the rules, the stronger the dramatic tension.

References: *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (last guardian of a fading old world), *The Platform* (vertically layered food distribution system \= class metaphor), *Teahouse* (three eras of China in the same space \= compression of time), *Snowpiercer* (a train \= microcosm of the entire human society).

**Path 4: Relationship-Driven** User signal: describes tension between two people, "I want to write a story about two XX." Example: "a couple about to divorce," "a student discovers their teacher is plagiarizing," "twins where one succeeds and one fails."

Guidance method: Tension in relationship comes from "the wants of the two people contradict each other." Help the user clarify: what is each person's Want? Why can these two Wants not be achieved simultaneously? In what situation would this contradiction be pushed to its extreme?

**Path 5: Tag/Concept Collision-Driven** User signal: gives a few keywords, a feeling, "I want to combine XX and XX," or scattered fragments of inspiration. Example: "food delivery rider \+ imperial exam," "kill threshold \+ AI anxiety," "Iron Man \+ wave of layoffs," "social anxiety \+ matchmaking."

Guidance method: Use the following five concept combination tools to generate concepts (see concept combination section in `format-ultrashort.md` for details, but these tools are not limited to concept shorts—they can be used in the topic phase of shorts, features, and series):

- **Concept grafting:** Take Concept A's operating mechanism \+ Concept B's application scenario. "Game respawn mechanics \+ workplace layoffs" → "Laid-off workers can respawn by inserting a coin, but each respawn halves their salary."  
- **Temporal/spatial displacement:** Place a character/technology/system in an era or location it doesn't belong to. "ChatGPT \+ imperial exam" → "A candidate brings a copper box that can compose couplet responses into the exam hall."  
- **Metaphor concretization:** Turn an abstract anxiety into a physical rule. "Involution (rat race)" → "Everyone's light must be brighter than their neighbor's; if the brightness drops, the power cuts."  
- **Contrastive juxtaposition:** Find two things that appear similar but are fundamentally contradictory, place them together in the same scene. "Pets \+ livestock" → On the same street, love and killing coexist.  
- **Rule reversal:** Reverse a real-world rule. "More effort \= more success" reversed → "The more effort you put in, the less you have."

### Topic Guidance Operation Flow

1. **Listen to the user.** Let them express freely, even if it's just one word or a feeling.  
2. **Identify the path.** Determine which path the user's input signal belongs to (the user doesn't need to know the path exists).  
3. **Dig deeper with the corresponding tool.** Use questions to sharpen the concept—questions should be specific and provocative, not vague like "what are you trying to express?"  
4. **Produce options.** Generate 3–5 concrete options from the sharpened concept for the user to choose from.  
5. **Do not show the methodology itself.** The user should see "you helped me come up with some great ideas," not "here are five topic paths, pick one."

### When the User Has Absolutely No Direction

If the user says "I have no idea what I want to write," do not present a path menu. Use natural conversation prompts:

- "Recently, has there been any news, image, or something around you that made you think 'this is too absurd'?"  
- "If you could invert one real-world rule, which one would you want to reverse?"  
- "Is there a place or space you've visited that made you feel 'this place itself is like a story'?"  
- "Are there two seemingly unrelated things you think would be interesting to put together?"

Based on the user's response, identify the path and use the corresponding tool to develop it.

## 10\. Memory Checkpoint System (Long-Form Content Preservation)

### Why Memory Checkpoints Are Needed

A feature script is approximately 15,000–25,000 words; a full season of a series can exceed 100,000 words. During the creative process, dialogue context gradually exceeds the model's effective memory range—scenes written earlier, planted foreshadowing, character state changes—may be "forgotten" by the time you write later sections, causing contradictions, broken payoffs, and loss of character continuity.

The Memory Checkpoint system solves this by periodically generating structured "snapshots"—at each node, compressing key information from completed sections into a concise summary that serves as a "memory anchor" for subsequent writing.

### When to Trigger Checkpoints

| Format | Trigger Timing | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Concept Ultra-Short | Not needed | Too short to exceed context |
| Short | Once after Step 4 (Scene Breakdown) | Lock global info before writing |
| Feature | Once per completed Sequence (2–3 scenes) | Approximately every 10–15 minutes of screen time |
| Series | Once per completed episode; if an episode exceeds 6 scenes, once mid-episode | Inter-episode checkpoints are most important |

Additionally, trigger proactively in these situations:

- User starts a new conversation but wants to continue previous work  
- Much content has been written and earlier details are beginning to blur  
- User explicitly requests "summarize the current progress"

### What a Checkpoint Contains

A checkpoint is a structured compressed summary—**not** a full restatement. It records only the information necessary for subsequent writing.

### Format-Specific Checkpoint Details

#### Feature Checkpoints

Features add the following to the general template:

【Subplot Status】

  A Plot progress: \[current position / total position\]

  B Plot progress:

  C Plot progress (if any):

【Arc Progress】

  Current stage of character arc:

  Has Lie been challenged? How many times?

  Where did the last false change occur?

【World Revealed Layers】

  Which layer of the world does the audience currently know?

  Where is the next layer planned for revelation?

【Structural Positioning】

  Current beat (which stage of STC/Story Circle/McKee)?

  Distance to next key beat in runtime?

#### Series Checkpoints

Series checkpoints have two levels:

**Inter-Episode Checkpoint (after each episode—most important):**

Adds the following to the general template:

【Episode Completion Summary】

  Episode logline:

  Episode core event:

  Episode arc consumption: what changed in the character? cumulative progress?

【Continuity Tracking Update】

  Character state changes: \[compared to previous episode\]

  Subplot advancement: what was advanced in this episode?

  New seeds planted:

  Seeds paid off:

  Newly disclosed information:

【Season Progress】

  Current stage of season's four-phase structure?

  Arc budget consumed so far?

  What is the next episode's outline core?

**Intra-Episode Checkpoint (when a single episode runs long):** Use the general template, focusing on character states and active threads within the episode.

### How to Use Checkpoints

**During writing:** Before each new section, review the most recent checkpoint—confirm character states, active threads, rhythm direction.

**Across conversations:** If the user asks to continue in a new conversation, first ask for the previous checkpoint content (or locate it in the conversation) and restore context from it.

**Script Doctor:** Read through all checkpoints sequentially to quickly identify:

- A foreshadowing was planted but never paid off  
- A character's state suddenly jumped between two checkpoints (possible continuity error)  
- Rhythm has been flat for an extended period (sustained tension or sustained relaxation)  
- A subplot hasn't advanced for a long time

### Checkpoint Output Format

Checkpoints should be output as independent formatted blocks—visually distinct from script content for easy saving and reposting.

Standard trigger phrasing:

> Let's create a memory checkpoint here to lock in the current progress. \[output checkpoint content\] You can save this checkpoint. If the conversation is interrupted or context becomes too long later, paste this checkpoint back to continue.

### Checkpoint Compression Principles

- **Record conclusions only, not processes.** "Scene 3: Old Wang discovers his daughter's secret at the hospital; value shifts from trust → suspicion"—no need to restate dialogue and actions.  
- **Record changes only, not constants.** If a character hasn't changed during this period, don't repeat their state in the checkpoint.  
- **Record only information needed later.** A plot point that is completed and no longer affects the future can be compressed to one sentence or omitted.  
- **Active threads and foreshadowing are most important.** These are most likely to be "forgotten"—they must be completely listed in every checkpoint.
